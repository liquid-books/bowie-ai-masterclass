> *"Culture does not change because we desire to change it. Culture changes when the organization is transformed — when organizations take new actions, new behaviors become the norm."*
> — Frances Hesselbein

Here is a scene that plays out in municipal governments all over Maryland, right now.

The City Manager sends an email to all staff: *"AI is a strategic priority for us. We are committed to responsible adoption. Training resources are available. Please reach out to your supervisor with questions."*

Ninety days later: nothing has changed. The permit application still gets processed by hand through the same five-person routing chain. The public works work order backlog still gets compiled from three separate spreadsheets every Monday morning. The quarterly constituent services report still takes two analysts two days to assemble.

Not because the employees don't care. Not because the tools don't work. Not because the strategy is wrong. But because **a message is not a movement**. An email is not a transformation. And announcing a priority is not the same thing as building a system for adopting it.

Chapter 2 gave you the personal operating system — the mindset. This chapter gives you the organizational operating system — the *change architecture*. How you take what you now believe about AI, what you've started to practice, and what you're beginning to build — and turn that into something that outlasts you. Something that spreads across a work crew, across a department, across all of City Hall.

Because the most important thing a City of Bowie employee can do with their AI capability is not just use it better. It is **multiply it** — across their team, their department, and their organization.

That is what this chapter is about.

## 1. The Burning Platform We Actually Have — Public Trust in the Age of AI

Most change management programs have to *manufacture* a sense of urgency. City of Bowie government does not. We are living inside one.

Maryland municipalities — and the residents who depend on them — are entering an era of rapidly shifting constituent expectations. Bowie's **70,000+ residents** increasingly expect the same speed and transparency from City Hall that they experience from their bank, their doctor's office, and their online accounts. Meanwhile, the cost pressures facing municipal government are intensifying: state aid formulas shift, infrastructure ages, and demands on services like public safety communications, parks maintenance, and constituent services grow every year.

Sit with what that actually means operationally.

The City's workforce is not growing at the same rate as constituent demand. The departments handling permits, public works requests, constituent inquiries, and code compliance are being asked to do more with the same or fewer resources. At the same time, the **Prince George's County** region is increasingly competitive for attracting and retaining professional talent. The City of Bowie cannot simply hire its way out of capacity constraints — not indefinitely, and not without careful stewardship of public funds.

That is the operational reality that makes AI adoption urgent rather than merely interesting.

:::{admonition} Why This Is the Right Moment — Not "A" Moment
:class: important

Kotter's first step in organizational change is *create a sense of urgency*. Most organizations manufacture it. Ours is structural and real.

There is a window that opens when leadership commits to transformation with genuine institutional support. The Mayor and City Council have endorsed the City's commitment to operational modernization. Department directors have budget authority to invest in workforce development. And — critically — residents are watching. The City's values of **Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, and Pride in public service** are not aspirational posters. They are the operational standard by which Bowie government is measured at every Council meeting, every resident forum, and every local news cycle.

AI adoption belongs inside that accountability framework — not as a tech novelty, but as a tool for delivering on those values more consistently and at greater scale.
:::

Here is the scale that makes this urgent rather than merely interesting. City of Bowie government touches the daily lives of **70,000+ residents** across every dimension of community life: the park where a family spends Saturday afternoon, the permit that allows a small business to open its doors, the pothole repaired before a resident's second complaint, the 911 call answered in seconds, the zoning question answered accurately the first time.

At that scale, a small improvement is not small. Twenty minutes saved per permit application, applied across hundreds of applications per year, is not a productivity tweak — it is a structural change in how quickly residents can build the lives they want in Bowie. One hour saved per public works work order summary, applied across every department, is human attention returned to the field work that actually requires judgment and physical presence.

That is the arithmetic of public-sector scale. AI does not have to be dramatic at the City of Bowie to be transformative. It only has to be *consistent*.

And we already have the proof that it works in contexts exactly like ours.

:::{admonition} The Proof Point From Our Peers — Municipal AI in Practice
:class: note

Across Maryland and the broader mid-Atlantic region, municipal governments are already deploying AI in ways that map directly to Bowie's operational context. Prince George's County agencies have piloted AI-assisted document drafting and constituent correspondence. Cities in Montgomery County have used AI tools for permit routing and public records request processing. The City of Baltimore has explored AI for internal workflow summarization.

Read those three capabilities again, because none of them are exotic. Document drafting drafts documents. Correspondence assistance helps with correspondence. Routing automation routes things. These are not moonshots. They are ordinary operational pain points that someone decided to stop tolerating.

That matters enormously for change management, for one reason: **it removes the "that wouldn't work in government" objection.** It already worked — in government, with government data, government workflows, and government constraints. When someone in City Hall says *"AI is for the private sector,"* the answer is not a statistic. It is: *"Baltimore tried it last year. Let me show you what they did."*
:::

## 2. Why Change Fails — And the One Thing That Works

Let us start with the uncomfortable data.

We already know from Chapter 2 that only **5% of organizations globally** qualify as "future-built" for AI — meaning they have genuinely restructured how they work, not just added AI tools to their existing processes. Meanwhile, 60% report minimal or no measurable value from their AI investments despite real financial commitment.

Why?

The answer is not technology. McKinsey's research is definitive on this point: **55% of organizations that are seeing AI returns redesigned their workflows.** They did not add Copilot to the old way of doing things and expect it to be transformative. They asked: *"Given that this tool exists, what is the new right way to do this work?"* And then they built that new way.

The organizations that are not seeing returns are, almost universally, running the old process with a new tool sitting next to it. Copilot open in one tab, the same manual workflow proceeding in another. The permit intake form still assembled by copying last year's version. The public works daily report still typed from scratch. The tool gets used for occasional convenience. The fundamental way of working never changes.

This is not a technology problem. It is a **workflow redesign problem** — which is, at its heart, a change management problem.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-why-change-fails.png
:label: fig-ch03-fails
:alt: Visual split showing why AI adoption fails — left side shows AI tool added on top of unchanged workflow with minimal results, right side shows AI tool integrated into redesigned workflow with exponential results — McKinsey research visual
:width: 80%
:align: center

The technology is not the variable. The workflow is. Organizations that redesign how work gets done — not just which tool they use — are the ones capturing exponential returns.
:::

Consider the shape this takes on a real City of Bowie workflow. The Planning & Community Development team handles zoning inquiries, permit applications, site plan reviews, and community outreach. Every one of those steps generates documents, emails, routing decisions, and response letters.

The tool-first version of AI adoption: every planner gets a Copilot license, and one person occasionally uses it to clean up an email.

The workflow-redesign version: the standard zoning response letter is generated from the application parameters rather than retyped from scratch; the weekly permit status report drafts itself from the database export and the planner edits it; the pre-application meeting summary pulls from OneNote notes into a structured record; the response to a constituent's inquiry starts as a first draft that the planner refines and approves rather than a blank page.

Same tool. Radically different outcome. The difference is entirely in whether someone stopped and redesigned the work.

The good news: workflow redesign does not require a top-down transformation initiative. It does not require a six-month consulting engagement. It does not require IT to rebuild anything.

It requires one person — perhaps you — to ask: *"What if we just didn't do it the old way anymore?"* And then to prove that the new way works on one real workflow. And then to show someone else.

That is how every lasting technology adoption in history has actually happened. Not from the top. From the middle. From the practitioners who tried something on a real case, discovered it worked, and couldn't stop talking about it.

## 3. Three Frameworks That Actually Work

Change management as a field has accumulated an enormous library of frameworks, most of which are useful in academic contexts and largely ignored in practice. We are going to focus on three — not because they are the most academically sophisticated, but because they map directly to what is actually happening when AI adoption succeeds or fails inside a public-sector organization with civil service structures, union agreements, and elected-official oversight.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-change-frameworks.png
:label: fig-ch03-frameworks
:alt: Three clean framework diagrams side by side — Kotter's 8-step staircase, ADKAR's five building blocks, and Bridges' Transitions Model showing the neutral zone — each with City of Bowie AI adoption annotations
:width: 80%
:align: center

Three frameworks, one purpose: making change stick instead of fade. Each illuminates a different dimension of the same transformation.
:::

### Kotter's 8-Step Model — The Organizational Staircase

John Kotter's framework, developed from studying hundreds of organizational transformations over 30 years, identifies eight conditions that must be met for change to take hold. Miss any one of them, and the change stalls. Understanding where your organization — or your department team — is on this staircase tells you exactly what to do next.

Municipal government adds a layer that private-sector frameworks sometimes skip: **legitimacy**. In a city government, urgency has to be authentic and grounded in public benefit, not competitive pressure. Vision has to survive a Council vote or at least a Council question. Coalition-building has to navigate departmental silos that reflect real statutory authorities, not just organizational charts. Change initiatives that don't acknowledge these realities either fail quietly or generate the kind of public controversy that sets the whole program back.

```{list-table} Kotter's 8 Steps — Applied to City of Bowie AI Adoption
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-kotter

* - Step
  - What It Means
  - What It Looks Like at the City of Bowie
* - 1. Create urgency
  - People must feel the need to change — not just be told about it
  - Residents expect modern, responsive city services. State technology mandates are evolving. Neighboring municipalities are piloting AI tools. The window to lead rather than follow is open now.
* - 2. Build a guiding coalition
  - Change requires a cross-functional group of champions, not just top-down mandate
  - Identify 3–5 respected employees across Planning, Public Works, Finance, Human Resources, and Constituent Services who believe in this and will model it
* - 3. Form a strategic vision
  - People need to see where they are going, not just what they are leaving behind
  - "In 12 months, every routine constituent response letter starts with a strong AI draft. Every departmental report begins from data, not a blank page. Every council briefing memo takes half the time it used to."
* - 4. Enlist a volunteer army
  - You need early adopters before you need everyone
  - Don't try to convert the skeptics first. Find the curious — the permit technician who already built a tracking spreadsheet everyone uses. Start there.
* - 5. Enable action by removing barriers
  - The biggest barrier is usually permission, not skill
  - Make it safe to experiment. Say out loud that using Copilot on a real work document is expected, not risky. Celebrate attempts, not just successes.
* - 6. Generate short-term wins
  - People need evidence it works before they commit
  - Identify two or three early workflow wins — a faster council memo, a cleaner public works report — and make them visible across departments.
* - 7. Sustain acceleration
  - Early wins get used to justify slowing down. Don't let that happen.
  - Use wins to expand across departments, not to declare victory after one success.
* - 8. Institute the change
  - Change becomes the new default — not a project, but the way we work
  - AI-assisted workflows are in departmental onboarding, in the supervisor checklist, in the standard operating procedure templates. New hires learn them on Day 1.
```

The most common failure point in AI adoption at government organizations is steps 4 and 5: organizations try to enlist everyone at once (skipping the volunteer army) and fail to remove the real barriers (usually permission and clarity about what's appropriate, not training). Fix those two steps and the rest follows faster than you expect.

Step 1 deserves a note about how urgency sounds in a public-sector context. Urgency is not fear. Telling a code enforcement officer that AI will make them obsolete is not urgency — it is a threat, and threats produce compliance at best and quiet grievance filings at worst. In a city government with civil service protections and union representation, that kind of communication can also create labor relations issues before the program has delivered a single result.

Real urgency at the City of Bowie sounds like this: *"Bowie's residents deserve city services that are as modern and responsive as the best governments in Maryland. AI is part of how we deliver on that — not to replace the people who do this work, but to give them better tools so we can serve 70,000 residents with the same dedication we always have, just faster and more consistently."*

### ADKAR — The Individual Change Model

Kotter describes the organizational staircase. ADKAR, developed by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci, describes the individual journey. Because organizations don't change — people do. And they change one at a time.

ADKAR is an acronym for the five things an individual needs before they will sustainably adopt a new behavior:

::::{card-carousel} 2

:::{card} **A — Awareness**
Understanding *why* the change is necessary. Not "AI is a strategic priority" — but *why*, specifically, your role and your work are affected and why now is the moment. For a permits coordinator, that means understanding how AI changes the application intake and routing cycle, not hearing a City Hall slogan.
:::

:::{card} **D — Desire**
Wanting to participate in the change. Awareness without desire produces compliance, not adoption. Desire comes from connecting the change to the person's own goals — fewer evenings spent reformatting the same report, fewer frustrated constituent calls, more time on the parts of the job they actually care about.
:::

:::{card} **K — Knowledge**
Knowing *how* to change. This is where most training programs start — and where ADKAR reminds us it's the third step, not the first. Skipping A and D and going straight to K is why most training doesn't stick.
:::

:::{card} **A — Ability**
Being able to apply the knowledge. There is a gap between knowing how to do something and being able to do it under real conditions — at a busy permit counter, fielding a constituent call, or preparing for a Council meeting that starts in two hours. Ability requires practice, feedback, and patience.
:::

:::{card} **R — Reinforcement**
Having the change sustained and recognized. Without reinforcement — positive feedback, recognition, peer visibility — new behaviors revert. Reinforcement is not a nice-to-have. It is what separates a training event from a culture shift.
:::

::::

When AI adoption stalls at the City of Bowie — in your department, on your team — diagnose which ADKAR stage is the bottleneck. Nine times out of ten, it is not Knowledge (people don't know how). It is Desire (people don't see why it matters to them personally) or Reinforcement (people tried it, it worked, and then nobody noticed).

Fix the right stage. Don't add more training when the problem is recognition.

There is a municipal-government-specific wrinkle here worth naming directly: **civil service and union dynamics affect every stage of ADKAR.** Awareness communications that sound like a management directive can trigger defensive postures in a unionized workforce before the program has even started. Desire cannot be manufactured through administrative pressure in an environment where civil service protections exist specifically to insulate professional judgment from political pressure. Reinforcement mechanisms that feel like surveillance — tracking who's using AI tools and who isn't — will generate union concerns before they generate adoption.

The ADKAR stages in a City of Bowie context must be built through *genuine peer-to-peer demonstration*, transparent communication with union representatives from the beginning (not after the fact), and a recognition culture that celebrates what employees accomplish with AI — not one that measures their AI utilization rate.

### Bridges' Transitions Model — The Emotional Truth

William Bridges made a distinction that most change management frameworks miss entirely: the difference between **change** and **transition**.

Change is the external event. A new tool is deployed. A new process is introduced. A City Council resolution directs the administration to modernize operations. Change happens on a calendar date.

Transition is the internal journey. It is how the people inside the organization move from the old way to the new one — emotionally, psychologically, professionally. Transition has three phases, and it does not start with the change. It starts with **endings**.

::::{admonition} The Three Phases of Transition
:class: note

**Phase 1: Endings.** Before anyone can fully commit to the new way, they have to acknowledge what they are leaving behind. The administrative specialist who spent fifteen years building expertise in the City's permit routing system — who people came to with questions, who was recognized across departments for their knowledge — is losing something real when AI can handle those routine routing decisions. That loss is real. Acknowledging it, rather than skipping past it with enthusiasm about modernization, is what makes the transition human and sustainable. This is **Accountability** and **Responsiveness** in the City's values applied inward: being honest with our own workforce about what this transition costs them emotionally, not just what it delivers technically.

**Phase 2: The Neutral Zone.** The old way is over and the new way is not yet fully real. People are in between. They know the old workflow, but it feels outdated. They are learning the new workflow, but they are not yet confident in it under the pressure of a Council deadline or a constituent standing at the counter. This is the most uncomfortable phase — and it is also where the most creative energy lives. The Neutral Zone is where departments figure out what the new way actually looks like in their specific context: Public Works scheduling is not the same as Planning permit intake, which is not the same as Finance budget reporting. It cannot be rushed.

**Phase 3: New Beginnings.** The new way becomes the default. The AI-assisted workflow is not the "AI workflow" anymore — it is just how the department operates. The beginning is complete when no one thinks of it as a transition anymore.

The leader's job is not to push people through these phases faster. It is to make each phase safe to inhabit.
::::

In a municipal government context, the Neutral Zone is particularly sensitive because public accountability doesn't pause while staff are learning. A city department cannot be in a "learning mode" when a resident needs a permit approved, when a Council member needs a briefing, or when a public works crew needs a work order to start their day. Change management for public-sector AI adoption has to be designed around the reality that the work continues — and that residents are watching — throughout the transition.

That is not a reason to delay. It is a reason to *stage* the transition carefully, starting with workflows where the cost of an imperfect AI output is low and the benefit of practice is high, before moving to the higher-stakes communications and documents where accuracy is non-negotiable.

## 4. Why AI Adoption Fails — The Five Organizational Failure Modes

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-failure-modes.png
:label: fig-ch03-failure-modes
:alt: Five organizational failure modes that kill AI adoption programs at public sector organizations
:width: 80%
:align: center

The five failure modes that consistently derail AI adoption at government organizations — each preventable with the right organizational design.
:::

With the frameworks in place, let us name the specific failure modes that appear most consistently in AI adoption programs at public-sector organizations. These are not theoretical. They are drawn from the pattern across government agencies, municipal departments, and public institutions that invested in AI tools and measured minimal returns.

**Failure Mode 1: Tool-First, Workflow-Never**
The City deploys the tool, runs a training webinar, and considers adoption complete. No one asks: *"What specific workflows are we redesigning?"* The tool becomes optional. The permit routing process is still the same twelve-step chain it was in 2018. The ROI doesn't materialize.

**Failure Mode 2: Compliance Without Conviction**
Employees complete the mandatory training, pass their certification, and never open the tool again. Because the training addressed Knowledge (ADKAR step 3) without ever building Awareness or Desire (steps 1 and 2). The training happened. The change did not. In a civil service environment, mandatory training completion is easy to achieve and means almost nothing on its own.

**Failure Mode 3: Champions Without Authority**
The City appoints a "City AI Champion" who is enthusiastic but has no team, no budget, no formal recognition, and no power to remove barriers — and who is also expected to keep up with their full workload. The champion burns out. The program fades. The lesson incorrectly drawn: "AI champions don't work." The correct lesson: champions without protected time and institutional authority are ceremonial, not structural.

**Failure Mode 4: Perfection as the Enemy of Progress**
IT and Legal spend six months building a perfect AI acceptable-use policy before anyone is allowed to experiment. By the time the policy is approved, the technology has moved twice and the policy is already outdated. Meanwhile, peer governments that started experimenting six months ago have six months of learning that we don't have.

**Failure Mode 5: Showcase Without Scale**
A brilliant early result is achieved — the Finance team redesigns their monthly budget variance narrative and saves four hours per report cycle. It wins internal recognition. And then it lives on a SharePoint page that nobody visits, and the Planning, Public Works, and Human Resources teams keep doing it the old way. Because the program never built a mechanism for taking individual wins and turning them into department-wide or city-wide standards.

Failure Mode 5 is the one City of Bowie should worry about most. Municipal organizations are structurally excellent at departmental problem-solving and structurally challenged at cross-departmental propagation. Every good idea in City Hall has to survive a silo test. If the mechanism for moving a workflow improvement from Finance to Planning to Public Works does not exist, the improvement stays in Finance.

::::{admonition} The Pattern Across All Five Failures
:class: important

In every case, the failure is not technical. The tool works. The employees are capable. The failure is a **systems failure** — a missing organizational mechanism for turning individual action into collective behavior. Awareness without desire. Training without practice. Showcases without scale. Champions without authority. Governance without permission.

The 5% of organizations that are "future-built" did one thing differently: they built the mechanism, not just the moment.
::::

## 5. Finding and Fueling the City AI Champions

Every successful bottom-up AI adoption story has the same cast of characters at its center: a small group of enthusiastic early adopters who were empowered to experiment, recognized when they succeeded, and given a visible platform to share what they learned.

These are your **City AI Champions** — department liaisons who serve as the human bridge between the tool and the team. Finding them is more important than almost any other organizational action in the early stages of the City's AI program.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-ai-champions.png
:label: fig-ch03-champions
:alt: Illustrated visual showing the City AI Champion profile — a professional at the intersection of three qualities: credible with peers, curious about technology, and willing to share failures as openly as wins — surrounded by ripple effects flowing outward through their team and department
:width: 80%
:align: center

City AI Champions are not the most technical employees in the room. They are the most trusted — and the most willing to learn publicly.
:::

A common mistake: organizations look for their most technical people to be AI Champions. This is almost always wrong. Technical sophistication is not the primary qualification. The qualities that make an effective City AI Champion are:

1. **Credibility with peers.** The Champion's colleagues already respect their professional judgment — they are the employee people go to when a constituent situation gets complicated, or the analyst whose numbers everyone trusts before they go to the Council. When they say, *"I tried this and it changed how I work,"* people listen, because they trust the source.

2. **Genuine curiosity.** Not enthusiasm manufactured for a role — but authentic interest in figuring out what is possible. You can hear it when you talk to them: they are asking questions, not just reporting answers.

3. **Willingness to learn publicly.** This is the rare quality. Most professionals are comfortable sharing successes. The Champion who says *"I tried this on the monthly public works report, it produced a mess, here's what I learned"* — in front of their team, without embarrassment — is the one who makes it safe for everyone else to try.

4. **A real workflow to test on.** The best Champions are not experimenting in the abstract. They are applying AI to actual city work — a real constituent response letter, a real department budget summary, a real council briefing memo — and reporting back from experience.

### Champions Must Span the Whole City, Not Just City Hall

This is where most AI programs at government organizations quietly fail. They recruit champions from administrative and finance functions — people who sit at desks, at computers, in offices — and then wonder why adoption never reaches the field employees.

City of Bowie cannot afford that mistake. A significant portion of our workforce does not spend its day at a desk. Public Works field crews, parks maintenance teams, Public Safety Communications dispatchers, building inspectors, and code enforcement officers experience their work through a radio, a tablet on a truck dashboard, or a handheld device in the field. They are the people delivering daily services to 70,000 residents, and they interact with technology differently than office-based staff.

A viable City of Bowie champion network needs representation from across operations:

```{list-table} Champion Coverage Map — Who Must Be Represented
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-champion-coverage

* - Department
  - Typical Work Context
  - What an AI Win Looks Like For Them
* - **Planning & Community Development**
  - Counter service, application intake, site plan review, constituent meetings
  - Zoning response letter first drafts; application status summaries; community meeting notes → action items
* - **Public Works & Engineering**
  - Field operations, project management, infrastructure records
  - Work order narrative drafts; inspection report summaries; project status updates for Council briefings
* - **Parks & Recreation**
  - Program scheduling, facility maintenance, community events
  - Program descriptions and marketing copy; maintenance request summaries; event logistics checklists
* - **Finance & Budget**
  - Budget development, financial reporting, audit support
  - Budget variance narratives; grant reporting first drafts; year-end summary preparation
* - **Human Resources**
  - Recruitment, employee relations, training, benefits administration
  - Job description drafts; policy update summaries; employee communication drafts
* - **Constituent Services**
  - Resident inquiries, request routing, council correspondence
  - Response letter drafts; request routing summaries; weekly inquiry trend reports
* - **Public Safety Communications**
  - 911 dispatch, radio operations (specialized, high-stakes environment)
  - Internal administrative documentation only — never operational AI in dispatch workflows
```

If your champion list has four people and all four work in administrative roles at City Hall, you do not have a champion network. You have a pilot for a small slice of City operations. Fix it before you launch.

### Reaching the Employees Who Don't Sit at a Desk

Change management for a field workforce is a different discipline than change management for office staff. A few principles that hold up in practice:

**Meet them in the rhythm of their workday, not City Hall's.** The morning crew briefing, the weekly supervisor meeting, the end-of-shift debrief. These already happen. A three-minute AI demonstration inside an existing meeting beats a 60-minute training nobody can attend from a field location.

**Mobile-first or it doesn't exist.** If the AI resource requires a desktop computer and a VPN connection, the field inspector will never see it. Copilot on a phone, a short video in Teams, a laminated one-page quick reference in the department truck — those travel.

**One workflow, not a curriculum.** A parks maintenance crew leader does not need an AI course. They need one thing: *"Dictate your end-of-day maintenance notes into your phone and let Copilot turn them into the formatted work completion report."* That is the whole intervention. It takes ninety seconds to demonstrate and it saves them significant administrative time.

**Respect the operational calendar.** Nobody is learning a new tool during the week of a major community event, a snowstorm public works response, or budget season's final push. Schedule adoption campaigns into the natural breathing room in the City's operational calendar.

### The Bottom-Up Pattern — How It Actually Happens

The most instructive case studies in public-sector AI adoption are not the top-down mandates. They are the grassroots ones — the ones that started with a single employee who tried something, and spread.

**ING** — one of Europe's most successful enterprise AI adopters — did not mandate adoption. They created communities of practice: informal groups where employees experimenting with AI could share what they were learning. No policy. No certification. Just structured sharing. Within 18 months, AI-assisted practices had spread to over 60% of their knowledge-worker population — driven almost entirely by peer-to-peer sharing.

This model translates directly to municipal government: **one identified AI enthusiast per department whose job is not to train colleagues but simply to be available, to demonstrate, and to celebrate when someone tried something and it worked.** This is the department liaison model — and it maps naturally onto Bowie's departmental structure. Every department has the right person. The question is whether the City gives them the time and visibility to play that role.

In a **union environment**, the bottom-up pattern has an additional advantage: **peer adoption is more credible than management mandate.** When a union member sees a colleague — someone in their same classification, with the same job duties, under the same collective bargaining agreement — using AI to save an hour per week, they are far more likely to try it than when a supervisor tells them to. The union model of peer solidarity, properly channeled, can be one of the most powerful accelerants in a public-sector AI adoption program.

That requires **early, transparent engagement with union leadership** — not after the program launches, but before. Union stewards and representatives who understand what the AI tools actually do, what the City's governance guardrails look like, and what protections exist against the tools being used to monitor or evaluate employees, are far more likely to be constructive participants than adversarial skeptics. Surprises generate grievances. Partnership generates adoption.

## 6. The Four Archetypes of Resistance

Not everyone is a Champion. And that is not a problem — it is a reality that effective change management accounts for rather than ignores.

Across public-sector organizations at different stages of AI adoption, resistant employees tend to cluster into four archetypes. Each archetype has a different underlying concern, and each requires a different response.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-resistance-archetypes.png
:label: fig-ch03-archetypes
:alt: Four-quadrant visual showing the resistance archetypes — The Cynic, The Perfectionist, The Territorial Expert, and The Overwhelmed — each in their own quadrant with a distinct icon and the right leadership move for each
:width: 80%
:align: center

Resistance is not uniform. The right move depends on which archetype you're dealing with. One response does not fit all four.
:::

### The Cynic

**What they say:** *"We tried something like this five years ago. There was a new system, a big rollout, a bunch of mandatory training. Six months later everything was back to how it was. This is the same story."*

**What they actually mean:** *"I have been burned before and I don't want to invest emotional energy in something that will be abandoned."*

**The right move:** Don't argue with the cynicism — validate it. Government organizations *have* hyped technology initiatives and abandoned them when leadership changed, budgets shifted, or the vendor contract expired. The Cynic's skepticism is not irrational — it is the accumulated experience of working in an institution where change is often announced and rarely sustained. What changes the Cynic is not more communication about strategy — it is a concrete, visible demonstration that this time is different. Show them a real workflow that actually changed. Show them the time saved and the constituent served better. The Cynic becomes a convert when the proof arrives. And when they convert, they become among the most credible advocates — because everyone knows they were the hardest to convince.

### The Perfectionist

**What they say:** *"I want to use it, but I need to make sure I'm doing it right. I'm not sending a resident an AI-drafted letter and having it be wrong. These are public records."*

**What they actually mean:** *"My standards are high, and I'm afraid AI output will undermine them — especially given the accountability this role carries."*

**The right move:** The Perfectionist is not resistant — they are appropriately risk-averse in an environment where errors in constituent communications, permit decisions, and public records are not just embarrassing but potentially legally and politically consequential. Give them structure: the verification discipline, the review protocol, the clear rule that AI drafts and humans decide and are accountable. Give them explicit permission to treat AI output as a first draft that they improve, not a finished product they endorse. This is **Accountability** in the City's values working alongside **Stewardship**: AI raises the floor, the employee still owns the ceiling and the outcome. The Perfectionist, once they see that AI raises their quality baseline rather than lowering their quality standard, often becomes an extremely disciplined and effective AI user.

### The Territorial Expert

**What they say:** (rarely out loud) *"If AI can do what I do, why does the City need me?"*

**What they actually mean:** *"My professional identity and my job security are connected to specific institutional knowledge — I'm the one who knows how the budget transfer process actually works, I'm the one who knows the history of that variance in the permit database, I'm the one who can navigate the Council's preferences — and this tool appears to be a direct threat to both."*

**The right move:** This is the identity layer from Chapter 2, manifesting at the individual level. In a civil service context, the concern can also be rational: employees who have seen positions reclassified or eliminated following technology changes have reason to be cautious. The right move is direct, honest acknowledgment: *"What AI is replacing is the mechanical part of your expertise — the transcription, the lookup, the reformatting. What it cannot replace is the institutional knowledge, the constituent relationships, the judgment about when a policy needs a human exception, and the accountability that comes with your position. Your value does not decrease when AI does the drafting. It increases — because now your judgment operates at a higher level with better inputs."*

Then prove it by giving them an AI tool that visibly amplifies their expertise rather than substituting for it. Better still: make them the person who *captures* their institutional knowledge into a shared knowledge base with AI's help. The Territorial Expert who becomes the author of the departmental procedure guide has not lost status — they have institutionalized it.

### The Overwhelmed

**What they say:** *"I want to learn this, I just don't have time. I've got two Council memos due, a grant report, and six constituent callbacks. Can we revisit after budget season?"*

**What they actually mean:** *"My capacity is genuinely at its limit and I need someone to make the on-ramp shorter."*

**The right move:** Reduce the barrier. Fifteen minutes, one use case, right now — not a training program. Not a certification. Not a self-directed learning module due in two weeks. Sit next to them, open Copilot, and apply it to something they are working on today: the memo due this week, the report due Friday. The Overwhelmed employee, once they experience the time savings themselves, becomes motivated to invest the learning time — because they have seen the return. The barrier is not willingness. It is the apparent size of the investment before the payoff.

The Overwhelmed archetype is the most common one at City of Bowie, and it is the least about resistance. These are your most dedicated people — the ones who stay late to get the Council packet right, who call constituents back same day, who know every nuance of their workflow. They are not saying no. They are saying *not like this*.

## 7. Change Management for Copilot Cowork — A Different Kind of Change

Everything up to this point applies to AI you *chat with*. There is now a second category, and it requires its own change management approach.

**Microsoft Copilot Cowork** reached general availability on **June 16, 2026**. It runs on Anthropic models and it does something categorically different from Copilot Chat: it executes **long-running, multi-step tasks end-to-end across Microsoft 365**. You describe an outcome; it works through the steps across Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, Teams, and SharePoint, and comes back with the result. It is billed on a **usage-based model, metered in Copilot Credits**, rather than as a flat per-seat license.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-communication-patterns.png
:label: fig-ch03-communication-patterns
:alt: Communication patterns that build trust during AI adoption at a city government
:width: 80%
:align: center

Effective AI change communication flows from City leadership through department heads, supervisors, and City AI Champions to every employee — in the field, at the counter, and at the desk — with feedback loops built in at every level.
:::

Why does this deserve its own section in a change management chapter? Because the behavioral shift is much larger than the one Copilot Chat required, and three specific things break if you roll it out the same way.

### Shift 1: From Asking to Delegating

Copilot Chat is a conversation. You ask, it answers, you keep control of every step. Cowork is a **delegation**. You hand over an outcome and step away.

That is a genuinely different professional muscle, and most people have never built it in a software context. The closest human analogue is assigning a task to a capable new employee: you have to specify the outcome clearly, provide the right context up front, agree on what "done" looks like, and then review the result properly rather than assuming it's complete.

Delegation is a *management* skill. Cowork asks individual contributors to use it, often for the first time.

In a **public-sector context**, delegation of multi-step work also carries accountability implications that don't exist in the same way in private industry. When a government employee delegates a task — even to a human colleague — accountability still rests with the delegating official. When a department director asks Cowork to draft a constituent response letter across multiple sources, that director is accountable for what gets sent, just as they would be if they had asked a staff member to draft it. Cowork does not change who owns the outcome. It changes who does the drafting.

::::{admonition} What Good Delegation Looks Like With Cowork
:class: tip

**Weak delegation:** *"Prepare the quarterly constituent services report."*

**Strong delegation:** *"Using the constituent inquiry log in our department SharePoint folder and the response time data from the past quarter, produce a summary report that lists the top five inquiry categories by volume, calculates the average response time for each, identifies any category where average response time exceeded our 48-hour standard, drafts a brief narrative explaining the top two variance drivers, and flags anything you could not verify from the source files. Do not estimate missing values — list them as data gaps requiring staff verification."*

The second version tells Cowork what "done" means, where the authoritative data lives, and — critically — what to do when it doesn't know something. That last instruction is the one people forget, and it is the one that protects the City's accuracy and accountability standards.
::::

**The change management implication:** teach delegation, not prompting. The training for Cowork is not a prompt library. It is a briefing discipline. If your team already knows how to write a clear scope of work for a contracted service, they already have the skill — they just need to see that it transfers.

### Shift 2: New Review and Approval Norms

When a person does the work, we know how to review it. We have established norms: a constituent letter goes to the supervisor before it goes out; a Council memo gets reviewed by the department director; a grant report gets checked by Finance before submission.

When Cowork does multi-step work autonomously, those norms need to be restated explicitly — because the work arrives looking finished. Polished output creates a false sense of completion. A well-formatted memo feels reviewed even when nobody has reviewed it.

In city government, this is not just an internal quality issue. AI-generated documents that go out as official City communications, are submitted to Council, or are filed as public records without proper human review and authorization carry institutional and potentially legal risk. Teams need clear answers to questions they have never had to ask before:

- Who reviews Cowork output before it becomes an official City document?
- Does an internal working document need the same review as a constituent-facing letter? (No — but say so explicitly, or people will either over-review everything or under-review everything.)
- How do we mark a document as AI-drafted so a reviewer knows what level of scrutiny to apply?
- What is the standard for verifying the *sources* Cowork used, not just the output it produced?
- Where does accountability sit? (Always with the human official who authorized it. Always. **Accountability** in the City's values is non-transferable — a tool cannot own a government decision or communication.)

:::{admonition} A Practical Review Standard for Cowork Output
:class: note

Adopt this as a department norm and write it down somewhere people will actually see it:

1. **Trace the sources.** Before reviewing the output, check which files, emails, and data it drew from. Wrong inputs produce confidently wrong outputs.
2. **Spot-check the figures.** Verify at least three data points against the source. If any fail, review the entire output, not just that figure.
3. **Read the gaps section.** If you instructed Cowork to flag what it couldn't verify, read that first. It is the most valuable part of the deliverable.
4. **Own it before you send it or file it.** The employee who delegated the task is the employee whose professional judgment is on the document. Nothing goes to a resident, the Council, or the public record without a named human who has read it end to end.
5. **Log the pattern.** If Cowork made an error likely to recur, capture that in your department's shared notes so the next person doesn't rediscover it the hard way.
:::

### Shift 3: Usage-Based Billing Requires Real Governance

This is the shift most organizations underestimate, and it is a genuine change management problem as well as a public stewardship problem.

For the City of Bowie, spending public funds requires proper authorization and transparency. Cowork's **Copilot Credits** model is usage-based: consumption varies by how much work you delegate and how large those tasks are. Unlike a flat per-seat license that fits neatly into the annual technology budget line, Cowork costs can vary with usage patterns in ways that require active monitoring.

Two failure modes follow, and they are opposites:

**Failure Mode A — Fear of the Meter.** Employees find out usage costs money and stop using it. Adoption dies quietly, and nobody escalates because nobody wants to be the person who ran up a technology bill. This is by far the more common outcome, and it is the more expensive one — the City paid for capability it never used.

**Failure Mode B — Uncontrolled Spend.** No guardrails, no visibility, no authorized budget owner. Consumption spikes in one department, Finance flags it at month-end, and the program gets suspended — which then produces Failure Mode A permanently, plus a new generation of Cynics who now have a story about government AI spending running amok.

The way through is explicit budget authority communicated *before* rollout:

```{list-table} Cowork Governance — Guardrails That Enable Rather Than Freeze
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-cowork-governance

* - Guardrail
  - What It Looks Like in Practice
  - Why It Matters for Adoption
* - **Named budget owner per department**
  - Each department director (or designee) is accountable for their credit pool — this is standard public-sector budget management applied to a new cost type
  - Removes ambiguity. No one has to guess whether they have authorization to use the tool.
* - **A stated monthly allowance per team or user**
  - "Your team has X credits this month. Use them on real work. If you exhaust them, tell us — that's a signal of value, not a violation."
  - Directly neutralizes fear of the meter. Permission is explicit and authorized.
* - **Visible consumption reporting to supervisors**
  - Monthly credit usage shared with department supervisors, with quarterly reporting to Finance
  - Supports public stewardship. Employees self-regulate when they can see. They worry when they can't.
* - **A short list of high-value approved use cases**
  - Constituent response drafting, Council memo preparation, grant report first drafts, departmental reporting
  - Directs authorized spend toward tasks with obvious public-service payback.
* - **A "when not to use Cowork" list**
  - Simple single-step drafts, quick research, anything Copilot Chat handles in thirty seconds; anything involving personal data, legal filings, or law enforcement records
  - Prevents burning multi-step credits on single-step work and keeps high-risk content in human hands.
* - **A no-blame escalation path**
  - "If you think a task is worth the spend and you're near your limit, ask your supervisor. No one gets in trouble for asking."
  - Keeps the highest-value work from being self-censored.
```

::::{admonition} The One Sentence That Prevents Failure Mode A
:class: important

Whoever launches Cowork to a City of Bowie department — a department director, a division supervisor, a City AI Champion — should say this out loud, in plain language, in the first five minutes:

> *"Yes, this is metered. Yes, it is in the authorized budget. Using it on real work is exactly what the appropriation is for. If you use your allocation doing legitimate city business, that is information we want — it means the tool is working — not a problem you caused."*

Without that sentence, a significant share of employees will quietly avoid the tool and never tell you why.
::::

### Sequencing Cowork Behind Chat

One last piece of practical sequencing: **do not lead with Cowork.**

Copilot Chat is the on-ramp. It is low-stakes, immediately useful, conversational, and forgiving. It builds the fundamental habit of turning to AI at the moment of work. Cowork is the second-story addition, and it lands well only on employees who have already built that habit and developed some judgment about where AI is reliable and where it isn't.

A reasonable City of Bowie sequence looks like this:

- **Months 1–3:** Copilot Chat across all departments. Build the habit. Establish verification discipline. Identify City AI Champions.
- **Months 4–5:** Cowork pilot with City AI Champions only — 6–10 employees across Planning, Public Works, Finance, HR, and Constituent Services. Real workflows, tracked outcomes, tracked credit consumption. Build the review norms *from what actually happens*, not from theory.
- **Month 6+:** Expand Cowork to department teams whose workflows the pilot proved out, with governance guardrails and the review standard already written, shared with union representatives, and approved by Finance.

Skipping the pilot and rolling Cowork to everyone at once is how you get both failure modes simultaneously — and how you turn a promising city initiative into a cautionary tale at the next Council work session.

## 8. Communication Patterns That Build Trust

The way City leadership talks about AI matters enormously — more than most leaders realize. The wrong communication pattern creates anxiety, breeds cynicism, and makes resistance archetypes entrench further. The right pattern builds the psychological safety that allows employees to experiment, learn, and grow in public service.

Here are the communication patterns that work — and the ones that undermine what they intend:

```{list-table} AI Communication — What Works vs. What Backfires
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-comms

* - What You Might Say
  - What Employees Hear
  - What to Say Instead
* - "AI is a strategic priority for the City."
  - "This is mandatory and you'd better comply."
  - "AI is a professional development opportunity I want to personally support — and I'm learning it alongside you."
* - "We need to stay ahead of the curve."
  - "We're behind and I'm worried."
  - "Bowie's residents deserve modern, responsive city services. AI is part of how we deliver on our values — Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, and Pride."
* - "This will make everyone more efficient."
  - "We're going to do the same work with fewer people."
  - "This takes the retyping, the reformatting, and the report assembly off your plate so you can focus on the work that actually requires your professional judgment and your relationship with this community."
* - "I expect everyone to complete the AI training."
  - "Check the box. Move on."
  - "I'm doing the training too. Here's what I learned this week. What did you learn?"
* - "Other cities are using AI."
  - "We're reacting to peer pressure."
  - "The employees who build AI habits now will serve Bowie's residents better — and will have skills that strengthen their careers in public service for years."
* - (Silence after a failed AI experiment)
  - "Trying and failing is not safe here."
  - "Tell me what happened. What would you do differently? Good attempt — that's exactly how we learn."
* - "Cowork is available to everyone starting Monday."
  - "Another tool. Another thing to learn during Council report season."
  - "Cowork handles the long, multi-step work — report assembly, grant draft preparation, multi-source summaries. Here's your authorized budget, here's who to ask, here's one task to try it on."
* - "This is a City-wide initiative."
  - "This is for City Hall, not for field crews."
  - "This is for every department — the parks crew, the building inspector, the 911 admin team — not just office staff. Here's the one thing it does for a field supervisor."
```

The most powerful communication pattern of all is the one that is hardest for most leaders: **public modeling**. Talking about your own AI experiments — what worked, what didn't, what surprised you — in department meetings, in one-on-ones, in briefings to the Council. Not as a performance of innovation, but as a genuine sharing of a professional learning process.

This is where leadership visibility does disproportionate work. When the City Manager frames AI as part of Bowie's commitment to excellent public service; when Department Directors each connect it to their department's specific mission; when City AI Champions share real examples from their own workflows in department briefings — the message stops being a City Hall memo and starts being a set of examples employees can follow.

Employees do not adopt what leaders announce. They adopt what leaders visibly do.

When leaders model publicly, they create permission. Permission for their team to try things that might not work. Permission to talk about AI as a real, evolving practice rather than a polished corporate deliverable. Permission to be in the Neutral Zone together, figuring it out collectively.

In a **union environment**, that permission is especially important to model at the supervisor level — because supervisors are the first line of interpretation when employees ask: *"Does the City actually want me to do this, or is this just another initiative that will disappear in six months?"* A supervisor who visibly uses AI tools, talks openly about what they're learning, and explicitly welcomes employee experimentation is worth more than any number of policy communications.

That permission is worth more than any training program you can buy.

## 9. The 100-Day Playbook

Every successful AI adoption program needs a structured beginning. Not because structure is more important than culture — but because structure creates the conditions in which culture can form. Without a defined sequence, adoption dissolves into the urgency of daily operations — and at the City of Bowie, daily operations include constituent demands, Council deadlines, infrastructure emergencies, and the rhythm of public service that doesn't pause for technology initiatives.

Here is the 100-day playbook, adapted for a municipal government with departmental silos, a mixed office-and-field workforce, union agreements, and public accountability:

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-100-day-playbook.png
:label: fig-ch03-playbook
:alt: Clean timeline infographic showing the 100-day AI adoption playbook in three phases — Days 1-30 Awareness (ignite curiosity), Days 31-60 Pilots (prove value), Days 61-100 Scale (spread what works) — with specific milestones and activities listed for each phase
:width: 80%
:align: center

The 100-day playbook. Three phases, one goal: transform AI from a topic people talk about into a practice people live.
:::

### Days 1–30: Awareness — Ignite the Curiosity

The goal of the first 30 days is not adoption. It is **curiosity**. You want employees asking questions, not completing checkboxes.

- **Week 1:** Leadership communication — personal, specific, and modeling-focused. Not a policy announcement — a genuine *"I tried this and here's what I found"* message from the most credible department voice available. Connect it to the City's values: this is about serving Bowie's residents better. **Before this communication goes out, brief union leadership** — a heads-up, not a negotiation, but a professional courtesy that prevents the program from starting with a labor relations surprise.
- **Week 2:** Identify City AI Champions. Three to five volunteers across different departments and position levels — and make sure at least two of them work outside a traditional office setting. Give them access, protected time (even an hour a week matters), and a clear mandate: experiment and share.
- **Week 3:** First "AI Open House" session — informal, one hour, hybrid so remote and field employees can join, recorded for staff who can't attend during their shift. City AI Champions demonstrate something real from their own work. No slides, no certification. Just: *"Here's what I tried on a real City workflow. Here's what happened. Here's what you can try today."*
- **Week 4:** Open sharing channel — a Teams channel, a SharePoint page — where employees can share AI experiments, tips, and questions. Seed it with Champion contributions. Make sharing feel low-stakes. Make sure it's accessible on a phone, because many employees will only ever see it on a mobile device.

### Days 31–60: Pilots — Prove Real Value

The goal of the second 30 days is **evidence**. Specific, measurable, visible results that make the case from the inside better than any external report.

- **Week 5–6:** City AI Champions each identify one repeatable workflow in their department and redesign it with AI assistance. Not a demo — a real workflow the team actually uses. Good candidates: constituent response letter drafting, weekly department activity reports, Council memo preparation, grant narrative first drafts, permit routing summaries, park facility maintenance logs.
- **Week 7:** First internal "what's working" session. Champions present: *"We changed how we do X. Here is what it looked like before. Here is what it looks like now. Here is the time saved / quality improved / stress reduced."* Share the results with department directors.
- **Week 8:** Expand access, and open the **Cowork pilot** with your Champion group if Chat habits are established. Set the authorized credit budget and the review norms before anyone starts, not after. Share the governance framework with Finance and union representatives before the pilot begins.

### Days 61–100: Scale — Spread What Works

The goal of the final 40 days is **institutionalization**. Making the AI-assisted workflow the default, not the exception — and, critically for the City of Bowie, making it travel across departments rather than staying in one division's knowledge base.

- **Week 9–10:** Document the redesigned workflows as standard operating procedures. Put them where staff actually look: the department procedure manual, the SharePoint site, the supervisor checklist, the new employee orientation materials. Make AI-assisted practice the baseline expectation for new hires.
- **Week 11–12:** First City-wide Showcase — internal, celebrated, visible, and **cross-departmental**. Invite Planning, Public Works, Finance, HR, Constituent Services, and Parks. This is your direct countermeasure to Failure Mode 5. A workflow improvement that only one department knows about is a rounding error; the same improvement adopted across all departments is a city-level result that shows up in the annual report and in resident satisfaction.
- **Week 13–14:** Review what the 100 days revealed. What worked? What stalled? Who surprised you? What did the Cowork pilot teach you about authorized budget, review norms, and where delegation genuinely pays? What should the next 100 days look like? Consider whether a brief report to the City Council on the program's early results would be appropriate — transparency about the investment and its returns is consistent with the City's accountability values.

## 10. Governance vs. Permission — Finding the Line Between Protection and Paralysis

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-governance-line.png
:label: fig-ch03-governance-line
:alt: The governance spectrum from paralysis by policy to reckless adoption, with the right line in the center
:width: 80%
:align: center

The governance spectrum: over-restriction kills adoption just as surely as reckless deployment breaks public trust. The right line is governed innovation.
:::

This is the hardest conversation in every AI adoption program in government, and it is worth having directly.

City of Bowie carries real obligations. We handle **resident personal information** — names, addresses, contact information, financial records, code enforcement histories. We operate under **Maryland public records law** where the content of our documents may be subject to public records requests. We communicate with **elected officials** whose correspondence may have political sensitivity. We work under **union collective bargaining agreements** where any change to working conditions requires appropriate process. We handle **financial data** that is subject to audit. We issue **permits, licenses, and code enforcement actions** that have legal standing.

That weight is real. The people who carry it are doing important work.

And here is the tension: **the same caution that protects the City from risk can, if miscalibrated, protect the City out of its ability to serve residents effectively.**

The question is not whether to govern AI use. Governance is non-negotiable in public-sector AI. The question is: **what kind of governance, applied where, at what stage?**

```{list-table} Governance That Enables vs. Governance That Paralyzes
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-governance

* - Governance That Enables
  - Governance That Paralyzes
* - "Here is what you are permitted to do. Start there."
  - "You must get approval before you do anything with AI."
* - "Here is the approved use case list. Help us add to it."
  - "AI use requires a formal risk assessment per workflow."
* - "Here is what not to put in AI — resident PII, personnel records, legal filings, union negotiation materials. Everything else is fair game."
  - "AI use is under review pending City Council approval of a policy."
* - Fast lane for low-risk experiments, scrutiny for high-risk ones
  - One governance process for all AI use regardless of risk level
* - Policies that evolve with the technology and with what we learn
  - Policies finalized before anyone has tried the tools in real work
* - "Go. Here are the guardrails."
  - "Wait. Here is the approval process."
* - Cowork credit budgets authorized in advance with a path to request more
  - Cowork access frozen until someone can guarantee exact annual consumption
```

The principle is **proportionality**: governance intensity should match risk level. Using Copilot to draft talking points for an internal department meeting is a completely different risk profile from using AI to generate a constituent-facing code enforcement notice, interpret a collective bargaining provision, or draft a public statement for the Mayor. Treat them differently. Reserve the full review apparatus for the workflows where it genuinely matters, and create a fast lane for the vast majority of daily AI use that is inherently low-risk.

A workable proportionality tier for City of Bowie:

```{list-table} Proportional Governance Tiers — A Starting Model
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-gov-tiers

* - Tier
  - Example Work
  - Governance Posture
* - **Green — Go**
  - Internal emails, meeting summaries, first-draft working documents, brainstorming, formatting, translation of internal materials, learning and practice
  - No pre-approval. Standard verification discipline applies. Use freely.
* - **Amber — Go, then review**
  - Constituent-facing correspondence, Council memos, grant application narratives, departmental reports, permit status communications, press releases for routine matters
  - Named supervisor reviewer before it goes out as official City communication. Sources verified, figures spot-checked.
* - **Red — Specialist involvement required**
  - Resident personal information (PII), personnel records, code enforcement legal notices, collective bargaining materials, legal filings, law enforcement records, published financial statements, anything with significant legal or political exposure
  - City Attorney, HR Director, Finance Director, or the relevant specialist in the loop by design. AI may assist in information gathering; humans make and document the decision.
```

The tiers matter more than their exact contents. What kills adoption is ambiguity — when an employee genuinely does not know whether using Copilot on the document in front of them is encouraged or a potential policy violation. In the absence of clarity, most professionals — especially in civil service, where professional caution is a reasonable career strategy — choose inaction. Publishing even an imperfect tier model beats publishing nothing, because it converts anxiety into a decision employees can make in five seconds.

The goal is: **go with guardrails**, not wait with approval.

## 11. Map Your Team's AI Readiness

Here is the tool that makes everything in this chapter actionable at the individual supervisor and department level.

The **AI Readiness 2×2** maps your team members across two dimensions: their current AI skill level (from low to high) and their willingness to change (from low to high). The four quadrants that result require four different leadership moves.

:::{figure} ../images/ch03-readiness-2x2.png
:label: fig-ch03-2x2
:alt: Clean 2x2 matrix diagram showing AI team readiness. X-axis: Willingness to Change (Low to High). Y-axis: AI Skill Level (Low to High). Four quadrants labeled: top-left = Coachable Stars (high skill, low willingness — need psychological safety), top-right = Champions (high skill, high willingness — deploy and amplify), bottom-left = Skeptics (low skill, low willingness — need evidence first), bottom-right = Eager Learners (low skill, high willingness — need structured access and quick wins)
:width: 80%
:align: center

Every person on your team fits somewhere in this matrix. Each quadrant requires a different leadership move. The same intervention applied uniformly to all four will fail for three of them.
:::

```{list-table} The AI Readiness 2×2 — Leadership Moves by Quadrant
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-2x2

* - Quadrant
  - Who They Are
  - What They Need
  - Your Move
* - **Champions** (High skill, High willingness)
  - Your early adopters — already experimenting, already building their own templates and shortcuts
  - Visibility, authority, protected time, a platform to share
  - Appoint them formally as City AI Champions. Give them time and recognition. Let them run the AI Open House sessions and the Cowork pilot.
* - **Eager Learners** (Low skill, High willingness)
  - Enthusiastic but untrained — asking questions, showing up to everything
  - Structured access, quick wins, a clear starting point
  - Give them Chapter 1 of this book. Sit beside them for 30 minutes on a live City workflow. Remove the blank-page barrier.
* - **Coachable Stars** (High skill, Low willingness)
  - Technically capable but emotionally resistant — often the Territorial Expert archetype
  - Psychological safety, acknowledgment of what they're protecting, visible proof
  - One-on-one conversation. Acknowledge what they're protecting. Show how AI amplifies their institutional expertise — and offer them the job of codifying it.
* - **Skeptics** (Low skill, Low willingness)
  - Haven't started and don't want to — often the Cynic or Overwhelmed archetypes
  - Evidence, not training. A win they can see, not a process they have to follow.
  - Don't start with them. Start with Champions. Let their results create pull. Give Skeptics time and peer proof.
```

The strategic insight in this matrix: **do not start with the Skeptics**. It is a natural impulse — the resistant are visible, and in a civil service environment, one vocal skeptic can create disproportionate headwinds, especially if they are a union steward or a long-tenured employee whose opinion others respect. But starting with skeptics is fighting uphill. Start with your Champions and Eager Learners. Build real results on real city work. Make those results visible across departments. The Skeptics will move when they see evidence — and some will not move until they see their colleagues succeeding without them, which creates the only motivation that works for the deeply resistant: social proof combined with a quiet sense that they're being left behind in building a skill their peers are acquiring.

## 12. The AI Readiness Questionnaire — Your Culture Diagnostic Tool

Everything in this chapter — the frameworks, the archetypes, the 100-day playbook — works only if you have an honest picture of where your team actually stands today. Not where you hope they are. Not the official narrative. The real picture.

That is what this diagnostic tool gives you.

The **AI Readiness and Integration Questionnaire** is a 15-question instrument designed to surface three things simultaneously: where each individual sits on the AI maturity spectrum, how the team perceives the City's AI capability, and where the highest-value AI improvement opportunities are hiding inside your department's day-to-day work.

:::{admonition} How to Use This Tool with Your Team
:class: tip

**Step 1 — Distribute individually.** Send the questionnaire to your team before a team meeting or session. Anonymous responses are fine — and often more honest, especially in a civil service environment where professional self-disclosure carries career implications. Give people 10 minutes to complete it on their own, in private. Make sure it works on a phone; many field employees will only ever access it that way.

**Step 2 — Present the aggregate results.** In your session, share the thematic patterns — not individual responses. "Several people identified permit routing as our biggest workflow bottleneck." "Most of us rated our AI skill at 3 or 4 out of 10." This creates a shared reality without exposing individuals.

**Step 3 — Use it as your implementation roadmap.** The responses to questions 10, 11, and 12 are not just survey data — they are a direct map to your first AI pilots. When multiple employees independently name the same pain point, that is your starting place.

**Step 4 — Repeat in 90 days.** Run the same questionnaire again. The shift in scores is your culture change metric — and, if you choose to share it with leadership, your program's evidence of progress.
:::

### Understanding the Five Dimensions the Questionnaire Measures

The 15 questions are not random. They are organized around five diagnostic dimensions, each revealing a different layer of readiness.

---

#### Dimension 1 — Personal AI Sovereignty (Questions 3, 4, 5)

*Where are you on the AI maturity scale?*

These questions assess your personal relationship with AI — not the City's, yours. This matters because organizational AI adoption is always, at its foundation, the aggregate of individual journeys. You cannot build an AI-capable municipal workforce from employees who have never personally experienced AI doing something useful for their specific job.

The AI maturity scale for individuals runs from five recognizable stages:

```{list-table} Personal AI Maturity Scale
:header-rows: 1
:label: table-ch03-ai-maturity

* - Level
  - Stage
  - What It Looks Like
  - Score Range
* - **1**
  - **AI Unaware**
  - Has not meaningfully engaged with AI tools. May have heard of ChatGPT but hasn't used it for real work.
  - 1–2
* - **2**
  - **AI Curious**
  - Has experimented — tried a few prompts, played with a chatbot, seen a demonstration. But nothing has changed how they work.
  - 3–4
* - **3**
  - **AI Functional**
  - Uses AI tools regularly for specific repeated tasks — cleaning up a constituent email, summarizing a long document thread. Has found at least one thing AI does reliably well. Getting value, but still dependent on the AI's defaults.
  - 5–6
* - **4**
  - **AI Integrated**
  - AI is embedded in daily workflow. Prompts intentionally. Has developed personal prompt approaches and workflows for their specific City work. Would feel meaningfully slower without it.
  - 7–8
* - **5**
  - **AI Sovereign**
  - Designs AI solutions. Delegates multi-step work to Cowork with confidence. Builds workflow automations and custom approaches. Thinks in systems, not just tasks. Could teach this to colleagues.
  - 9–10
```

Most City of Bowie employees will score between a 3 and a 5 — functionally aware, selectively capable, but not yet integrated into daily work. That is not a failure. It is the starting point. The gap between a 4 and a 7 is not talent — it is deliberate practice, structured exposure, and a permission environment that says it is safe to experiment on real City work.

Question 4 — *What's the most useful thing you've done with AI that actually changed how you work?* — is the most revealing question in the instrument. An honest answer reveals whether someone has crossed the threshold from curiosity to functional use. If someone cannot answer it, their score is probably a 3 or below, regardless of what they selected in question 3.

Question 5 — *What's been your biggest barrier?* — tells you what is between your team and their next level. Not enough time (especially during peak operational periods), not knowing where to start with city workflows specifically, distrust of AI outputs for official documents, unclear policy about what's permitted — each answer requires a different intervention. This is where City AI Champions become critical: peer-to-peer credibility removes barriers that no training deck can touch.

---

#### Dimension 2 — Organizational AI Perception (Questions 6, 7, 8)

*How does your team see the City's AI readiness?*

These three questions reveal something leaders often do not know: the gap between the official AI narrative and the experienced reality. A city government can have an announced AI initiative and still have a workforce that experiences the environment as unclear, unsupported, or potentially threatening to their positions. That gap is a trust problem, and it is a change management problem before it is a technology problem.

Question 6 asks what percentage of the team is genuinely AI-capable. If City AI Champions are working in isolation — getting results but not sharing them — their colleagues will underestimate the actual capability level of the team. Peer visibility matters enormously in a civil service environment where professional norms are set by what colleagues do, not what management announces.

Question 7 assesses whether employees experience the City's AI direction as real or rhetorical. The difference between "we're exploring some options" and "we have a program, a budget, and visible leadership support" is not semantics — it is the difference between employees feeling they have institutional backing for experimentation or that they are proceeding at personal professional risk in the absence of clear authorization.

Question 8 — the Bus Factor question — is the most uncomfortable one in the instrument for government organizations. *If your top three people in this area left tomorrow, what would be lost?* At the City of Bowie the honest answers often reveal significant institutional fragility: *"Only one person knows how the permit database really works." "The institutional history of the council district's zoning variances lives in one senior planner's memory." "The relationship with that community organization exists because of one employee."*

This question surfaces something AI cannot fix by itself: the organizational fragility of undocumented institutional knowledge built up over decades of public service. But it is also the clearest indicator of where AI-powered documentation, knowledge capture, and process codification would create the most immediate value for the City's continuity of service — independent of any particular employee's continued employment.

---

#### Dimension 3 — Role Context (Question 9)

*What is your role?*

Inside City of Bowie government, the relevant variable is function and work context. A planning technician, a parks maintenance crew leader, a budget analyst, a constituent services coordinator, and a public safety communications administrator all work for the City, but they have radically different AI use cases, different risk tolerances for AI outputs, different device realities, and different workflows where AI creates value.

Segmenting responses by role allows targeted analysis: where are Planning staff experiencing friction? What are Public Works field crews naming as their biggest administrative burden? What do Finance staff need that is different from what HR needs? Role-based patterns produce role-specific pilot candidates — and they prevent the most common rollout error, which is designing the whole program for the employees who happen to sit in City Hall offices.

---

#### Dimension 4 — Pain Points Addressable by AI (Question 10)

*What workflows are high-volume, time-consuming, or error-prone?*

This is the question that separates a diagnostic survey from actionable intelligence. Question 10 asks employees to describe 2–3 critical workflows that are either high-volume, error-prone, or require significant time or expertise to execute — specifically those with the most manual steps or where errors have real consequences for residents.

At City of Bowie, expect clusters around: constituent correspondence and inquiry routing; permit application intake and status communications; Council memo and briefing preparation; grant reporting and documentation; departmental reporting across Finance, HR, and Planning; and the various coordination activities between field crews and administrative staff in Public Works and Parks.

The responses to this question are a crowdsourced AI opportunity map. When multiple employees from different roles and different departments identify the same workflow as painful, that convergence is your highest-confidence pilot candidate — and it is also your best scale candidate, because a fix that matters in Planning probably matters in Finance and Constituent Services as well.

---

#### Dimension 5 — AI Automation Opportunities (Questions 11, 12)

*Where would AI create the most value if it worked perfectly?*

Questions 11 and 12 access something that structured workflow analysis often misses: employee aspiration and frustration. Question 11 — *If you could have AI handle one thing perfectly starting tomorrow, what would it be?* — reveals where employees are most burdened by tasks that feel beneath their professional capability. The framing removes pragmatic filters and gets to the honest answer.

Question 12 asks for the single biggest bottleneck or process inefficiency, with an estimate of time lost per week or month. This question produces the data you need to build a business case — framed not in private-sector ROI terms, but in public-service terms: *"How many more constituents could we serve if this administrative bottleneck were eliminated?"* When employees themselves are estimating the cost of a broken process, the case for investment becomes grounded in lived experience rather than outside consultant projections.

Together, questions 11 and 12 give you the data to answer the three questions every City AI implementation requires: *Where does AI create the most value for our residents? What does success look like in our department? How do we measure it in a way that would be meaningful to our Council and community?*

---

### Conducting a Thematic Analysis — Turning Responses Into a Culture Change Roadmap

The questionnaire is most powerful when aggregated. Here is how to run a basic thematic analysis that turns individual responses into a departmental or city-wide action plan.

:::{admonition} Thematic Analysis Protocol (45 minutes)
:class: note

**Step 1 — Score the Maturity Distribution (10 min)**
Tally question 3 responses. Plot your team on the maturity scale. What is the median? What is the range? Are most people at Level 2 (Curious) or Level 3 (Functional)? This tells you the appropriate starting point for your 100-day plan — and whether a Cowork pilot is premature or well-timed.

**Step 2 — Surface the Perception Gap (5 min)**
Compare question 6 and 7 responses to what you know to be true as a leader. If employees perceive lower capability or weaker organizational support than you know exists — if no one mentions City leadership's commitment to this program — you have a communication gap. That is a leadership action item, not a training action item.

**Step 3 — Map the Pain Points (15 min)**
Read all responses to question 10. Highlight recurring themes. Write each unique workflow on a sticky note or slide. Group them. The clusters that emerge — especially those appearing across multiple roles and multiple departments — are your pilot candidates.

**Step 4 — Identify the Top 3 Automation Opportunities (10 min)**
Read questions 11 and 12 together. Look for convergence. Where do employees' high-value wishes align with their identified bottlenecks? That intersection is your highest-confidence AI implementation target.

**Step 5 — Build Your Implementation Priority Matrix (5 min)**
Plot your top 3–5 opportunities on a simple 2×2: Impact (High/Low) vs. Feasibility (High/Low). Start with High Impact, High Feasibility — these are your Day 1–30 pilots in the 100-day playbook. Add a third filter: *does this scale across departments?* A fix that matters in Planning but nowhere else is worth less than a fix that helps Planning, Finance, and Constituent Services simultaneously.
:::

The goal of this exercise is not a perfect analysis. It is a shared, evidence-based starting point. When a team has collectively named its pain points, its aspirations, and its capability gaps — and those responses are reflected back as a group — something important happens: employees feel heard, and the AI initiative stops feeling like something being done to them. It starts feeling like something they helped design.

That shift — from AI as imposition to AI as response — is the foundation of sustainable culture change. It is also **Responsiveness** in the City's values expressed as an operating practice rather than a poster: listening to our own employees with the same attentiveness we want them to show to our residents.

## Chapter Summary

Change management is not the soft side of AI adoption. It is the whole game.

The technology works. City of Bowie employees are capable. The question — always the question — is whether the organizational conditions exist for individual action to become collective behavior. Whether the City AI Champions have platforms and protected time. Whether the permission is real and explicit. Whether the governance enables rather than paralyzes. Whether the message reaches the field crew and not just the department director's office. Whether leaders are modeling rather than mandating.

Municipal government change management carries stakes that private-sector programs do not. We serve a public that trusts us with their tax dollars, their safety, their neighborhood's future, and their daily quality of life. We operate under civil service protections and union agreements that exist for important historical reasons. We answer to elected officials who are themselves accountable to 70,000 residents. We are stewards of public resources, not managers of private capital.

Those realities are not obstacles to AI adoption. They are the context that makes it matter more — because when City of Bowie employees bring AI to their work, the beneficiaries are not shareholders or customers. They are neighbors. Families. The resident who needed a permit answered faster, the pothole reported by phone and fixed before the week was out, the constituent inquiry resolved on the first contact.

The City's values — **Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, and Pride in public service** — map directly onto what responsible AI adoption requires. *Accountability*: verify the AI's output honestly before it becomes an official City communication. *Responsiveness*: use AI to do more for residents, not less. *Stewardship*: authorize the investment wisely, govern the use carefully, protect resident data. *Pride in public service*: let AI handle the administrative burden so employees can do the work they chose this career for.

That culture is not an obstacle to AI adoption. It is the raw material that makes AI adoption sustainable — because when professionals with City of Bowie standards apply AI to their work, they do not lower their standards to match the AI's output. They raise the AI's output to match their standards.

The frameworks in this chapter — Kotter, ADKAR, Bridges — are not the answer. They are the map. You are the navigator. And the destination is a City of Bowie where AI-empowered public servants are the standard, not the exception — in City Hall and in the field, at the permit counter and in the public works yard, answering a Council question and answering a resident's call.

The 100 days starts whenever you decide it starts.

```{glossary}
City AI Champion
  A credible, curious, and publicly-learning City of Bowie employee who models AI adoption within their team or department — making it safe for colleagues to experiment by sharing both successes and failures openly. Serves as the human bridge between the AI tools and the workforce. Distinguished from a technical expert by professional credibility and peer trust, not technology sophistication.

ADKAR
  A change management model (Prosci) describing the five individual conditions required for sustainable behavior change: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. In a civil service and union context, all five stages require peer-driven approaches rather than management mandates.

Kotter's 8-Step Model
  A framework for organizational transformation identifying eight sequential conditions required for change to take hold — from creating urgency to institutionalizing the new behavior. Applied to municipal government, it must account for the legitimacy dimension: urgency grounded in public benefit, coalition-building across departmental statutory lines, and vision that can survive elected-official scrutiny.

Bridges' Transitions Model
  A change model distinguishing between external change (events) and internal transition (the psychological journey) — comprising three phases: Endings, the Neutral Zone, and New Beginnings. In public-sector adoption, the Neutral Zone is particularly sensitive because resident-facing services and public accountability continue throughout.

AI Readiness 2×2
  A team diagnostic tool mapping employees on two axes (AI skill level and willingness to change) to identify Champions, Eager Learners, Coachable Stars, and Skeptics — each requiring different leadership responses. In a civil service context, the matrix must be used to inform tailored outreach, not to create visible classifications that could affect how employees perceive their standing.

Permission Gap
  The organizational condition in which employees want to engage with AI but have not received clear, explicit authorization to experiment, fail, and learn — particularly consequential in civil service environments where professional caution in the absence of clear policy is a rational career strategy.

Proportional Governance
  The principle that AI governance intensity should match the risk level of the specific use case. Applying the same review process to an internal working document and an official code enforcement notice is not governance — it is bureaucracy that stops both equally. Green, Amber, and Red tiers allow appropriate protection without universal paralysis.

Floor Captain Model / Department Liaison Model
  An AI adoption approach in which identified enthusiasts are available within each department or work crew whose job is not to train colleagues but simply to be available, to demonstrate, and to celebrate when someone tried something and it worked. In a union environment, peer adoption driven by respected colleagues is more credible and more durable than management-driven training programs.

Copilot Cowork
  Microsoft's agentic Copilot capability (generally available June 16, 2026), running on Anthropic models, that executes long-running multi-step tasks end-to-end across Microsoft 365. In city government, its adoption requires explicit budget authorization, designated supervisory reviewers for official outputs, and the clear principle that the employee who delegates a task remains accountable for the result.

Copilot Credits
  The consumption unit used to meter Copilot Cowork usage. Because cost varies with how much work is delegated, city governments need named budget owners with Finance Department authorization, stated allowances per department or team, and visible consumption reporting to fulfill public stewardship obligations and prevent both fear-driven under-use and unauthorized overspending.

Delegation Standard
  A short, written department norm defining which multi-step tasks are appropriate to hand to an AI agent end-to-end, which are not, how tasks are briefed (sources, definition of done, instruction to flag unverifiable items and sensitive content), and who reviews the output before it becomes an official City communication or public record.

City Values (Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, Pride)
  The City of Bowie's core operational values — which map directly onto responsible AI practice: verify output honestly before it becomes official (Accountability); use AI to serve residents faster and better (Responsiveness); govern the investment carefully and protect resident data (Stewardship); bring AI to the work you chose public service for (Pride).
```
