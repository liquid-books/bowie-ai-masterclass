# Chapter 10: Week 3, Session C — Copilot in OneNote

Copilot in OneNote changes how City of Bowie employees capture, organize, and act on what they learn in the field. Four confirmed capabilities — summarize, create task lists, rewrite, and draft new content — work on the actual pages in your notebook, turning walkthrough scribbles and field observations into structured, actionable records.

> *"Notes are only as valuable as the action they generate. Copilot closes the gap between capturing and acting."*

Think about the last park facility walkthrough or public works field inspection you completed.

You were standing up. Clipboard under one arm, phone in your hand, the facility manager walking fast and talking faster. You learned that the pavilion roof at Allen Pond Park has a drainage issue nobody has documented officially, that the storm drain on Northview Drive has a capacity constraint that doesn't appear in any current infrastructure plan, that a contractor's crew was missing the correct safety signage, and that the electrical panel in the maintenance shed was flagged by a prior inspector but the corrective action was never formally closed. You typed some of it. You photographed the drain. You wrote three words on a printed site map and shoved it in your bag.

Now think about what happened to all of that.

For most people in city government the honest answer is uncomfortable. Some of it made it into an email. Some lived in your head until you moved to another project and left with you. Most went nowhere. Eighteen months later the same facility comes up for its next inspection cycle, a different Public Works team runs it, and somebody discovers the drainage problem all over again — on the day a complaint lands in the Mayor's office.

That is not a note-taking problem. It is an institutional memory problem, and it is the most expensive knowledge failure in municipal government.

Copilot in OneNote is built to close that gap — not the act of taking notes, which OneNote always did, but the distance between capturing something and being able to use it. From a page of field observations to a punch list with owners. From shorthand scrawled at a park maintenance site to documentation a colleague can act on. From four years of scattered facility notes to an answer in ten seconds.

Microsoft's documentation confirms Copilot in OneNote runs on Windows, Mac, iPad, and the web with a Microsoft 365 Copilot (Work) license, and on Windows with eligible M365 subscription plans. That coverage matters more here than almost anywhere. **Much of the City of Bowie workforce is not at a desk.** Public Works inspectors, Parks & Recreation staff, code compliance officers, and field crews are out in the community — in parks, on road rights-of-way, at construction sites, in neighborhoods across Bowie's 16+ square miles. A tool that only works on a laptop in City Hall is a tool most of these employees cannot use. OneNote is not that tool.

## 1. Why Notes Matter More in the AI Era, Not Less

There is a tempting misconception: if AI can summarize and recap automatically, why bother taking careful notes at all?

This gets the relationship backwards. **The quality of Copilot's output is directly proportional to the quality of the notes it works with.** Hand the best project manager at City Hall a napkin with three illegible bullets and their output is limited by what you gave them. Hand them a field inspection report with measured dimensions, named responsible parties, and specific code violation references, and you get something useful. Copilot organizes, synthesizes, and extends. It does not manufacture facts you never captured.

So the discipline matters more now, not less. A Public Works inspector who captures structured field notes has an asset Copilot turns into punch lists, escalation summaries, and a lessons-learned document that outlives the project. A department director whose program notebook holds four years of project history can be brought current on an initiative in ninety seconds.

OneNote was always the right tool for structured capture. Copilot does not change what OneNote is for. It multiplies the return on the time you already spend using it.

## 2. OneNote in the M365 Stack — Where It Fits and Why It Is Underused

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-onenote-m365-stack.png
:label: fig-ch10-m365-stack
:alt: Layered diagram showing OneNote's position in the Microsoft 365 ecosystem — Teams and Outlook at the communication layer, OneNote highlighted as the knowledge capture layer in the middle, SharePoint and OneDrive at the storage layer, with bidirectional arrows showing how meeting notes flow from Teams into OneNote, documents sync to SharePoint, and Copilot works within the notebook context
:width: 80%
:align: center

OneNote occupies the knowledge capture layer of the Microsoft 365 stack — between the communication tools above it and the storage infrastructure below. That placement makes it the connective tissue of the M365 knowledge ecosystem, and the place where what happens in the field turns into something the City can keep.
:::

Microsoft 365 is a knowledge ecosystem, not a bag of apps. **Teams and Outlook** are the communication layer — ephemeral by design. A Teams chat scrolls away; a project kickoff call ends and the conversation dissipates. **SharePoint and OneDrive** are the storage layer, where finished documents live: ordinances, policies, permits, SOPs, infrastructure plans, contracts. Excellent at retrieval, poor at the messy, iterative work of capturing knowledge while it is still forming.

**OneNote** is the knowledge capture layer in between — where the ephemeral gets distilled into something structured and persistent, and where the raw material for formal documentation gets organized before it is ready for final form. It is a notebook, not a document. Flexible, hierarchical, permanent.

In practice, most City of Bowie teams use Teams and SharePoint constantly and OneNote barely. The reasons are familiar: "I keep notes in email," "I have my own system," "I never set it up," "I'm never at a computer anyway."

Those are the answers you would expect from people who have never had an AI assistant living inside their notebook. A notebook that syncs across devices was never quite compelling enough to displace an existing habit. A notebook that also reads everything you have written, summarizes it on demand, extracts your commitments, and makes four years of facility history searchable is a categorically different proposition. The setup cost is one afternoon. The payoff compounds every project cycle.

## 3. How to Access Copilot in OneNote — Including on a Phone

Microsoft's documentation confirms three access points, each suited to a different working moment.

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-copilot-pane-onenote.png
:label: fig-ch10-copilot-pane
:alt: Illustration of the Copilot pane inside Microsoft OneNote — the left side shows a OneNote page with structured field inspection notes in bullet form, the right side shows the Copilot sidebar pane with a chat interface, prompt input box at the bottom, and AI-generated summary output above, with labels pointing to the Copilot Pane, Prompt Input Box, Generated Summary Output, and Page Context Indicator
:width: 80%
:align: center

The Copilot pane opens on the right side of OneNote from the Home tab. From here you can enter prompts, get summaries and task lists, chat about your notes, and copy the output straight into another page, an email, or a Teams message.
:::

**The Ribbon Button (Home Tab).** The primary access point. Click Copilot and the pane opens on the right while your page stays visible — the full interface, for longer summaries, multi-step requests, and iterative conversation.

**The On-Canvas OneNote Icon.** Triggers specific functions — summaries, rewrites, task lists, new content ideas — from the page context without opening the full pane. As of December 2025, according to SuperSimple365's tracking of M365 feature rollouts, "Summarise Page," "Create a Task List," and "Rewrite Page" now open directly in the Copilot Chat pane.

**Right-Click Context Menu.** The fastest route to common operations.

One constraint from Microsoft's documentation: Copilot in OneNote is **not available if Windows Information Protection (WIP) is enabled** on your device. On a managed device with WIP active, check with IT.

### The Mobile Reality

Here is the part that matters most for City of Bowie field staff, and it is in almost no Copilot training material. Notes in field-facing roles are not taken at desks. They are taken standing up, at a park facility, on a gravel road, on a phone, with one hand, while inspecting an infrastructure issue. If your capture habit requires sitting down at City Hall, you do not have a capture habit.

**Photograph the physical artifact.** Marked-up site plans, the whiteboard in the Parks maintenance office, a handwritten work order taped inside a utility room, a damaged sidewalk panel, a drainage structure that doesn't match the engineering drawings. The photo *is* the note; the one-line caption you type under it is what makes it findable later.

**Use ink freely.** If you work from an iPad with a pencil during walkthroughs — many code compliance officers and facility managers do — Microsoft confirms Copilot works with **both typed and handwritten notes on the same page**. Sketch the site layout, annotate it, and Copilot still summarizes it alongside your typed content.

**Talk your notes.** Walking from a park maintenance site back to your vehicle is four minutes of dead time that can become the day's field log. Dictate roughly, capture everything, clean it up later with the rewrite function in Section 5. For anyone whose job happens on their feet, this is the highest-leverage habit in the chapter.

The through-line: **capture fast and messy in the field, structure it later with Copilot.** Before AI, messy capture stayed messy, so people either wrote nothing or wasted time writing neatly in difficult conditions. That trade-off is gone.

:::{tip}
**The four-minute rule.** Any walk longer than four minutes — park pavilion to vehicle, construction site to office, Allen Pond to the maintenance yard — is a capture window. Open the page, hit dictate, and empty your head. It will be rough. Copilot cleans up rough. It cannot recover forgotten.
:::

## 4. Capability 1 — Summarizing Your Notes

Summarization is the capability that most immediately changes your relationship with your own notes, and one feature makes it far more useful professionally: **when Copilot summarizes your notes, it cites its sources within the response.**

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-summarize-chaos-to-clarity.png
:label: fig-ch10-summarize
:alt: Before/after transformation diagram showing raw field inspection notes on the left labeled 'Chaos' — scattered bullets, incomplete sentences, timestamps, tangential remarks — and a clean Copilot-generated summary on the right labeled 'Clarity' with sections for Key Findings, Action Items, and Open Questions, bridged by a large blue arrow labeled 'Copilot Summarize'
:width: 80%
:align: center

Raw field inspection and facility walkthrough notes become structured summaries with findings, open questions, and action items — with Copilot citing the specific parts of your notes it drew from, so you can verify before anyone acts on it.
:::

That citation mechanism is not a detail. In city government, a wrong number or missed code reference is not an inconvenience — it is a failed inspection, a liability exposure, or a resident complaint that escalates to the Council. When Copilot tells you which part of your page a point came from, you can check it.

Microsoft's documentation describes three levels of granularity: the **full page**, **a section or topic** (useful when an inspection page mixes infrastructure conditions with code violations and different staff need different parts), and **a selected passage** (surgical, ideal for dense material like a venue's permit conditions or a contractor's work specifications).

Microsoft's own example prompt is instructive: *"Create a summary of the notes on this page. Focus the summary on the chronological order of events and place the events into bullet points."* Naming a focus and a format separates a useful summary from a generic one. **The more specific your instruction, the more useful the output.** A **copy button** then moves the result anywhere — an email to a department director, a Word doc for the project file, the project's Teams channel, a new recap page.

### City of Bowie Application: The Field Inspection Debrief

A Public Works inspector finishes a two-hour walkthrough of infrastructure along a corridor scheduled for maintenance in eight weeks. The page holds drainage structure conditions and sizing notes, the pavement distress inventory, utility marking locations that differ from the GIS drawings, marshaling constraints for contractor equipment access, contractor jurisdiction notes from a pre-construction meeting, two photos of a blocked right-of-way, and an ink sketch of a revised traffic control layout for one block.

She prompts: *"Summarize this page. Organize into: Drainage and Stormwater Conditions, Pavement Conditions, Utility Conflicts, Traffic Control Implications, and Open Items. Use bullet points. Note anything that conflicts with the current GIS drawings."*

Thirty seconds later she has a five-part debrief. She checks the citations, corrects one drainage structure number, and copies it into the project's Teams channel and the facility notebook. The debrief that would normally have been written at 10 p.m. from memory is done before she leaves the site — and it is more accurate, because it was written while she was standing in it.

## 5. Capability 2 — Rewriting Notes for Clarity

The second capability turns rough, informal, incomplete notes into clear documentation — resolving the oldest tension in field note-taking: speed versus quality. During a site visit the right posture is fast and comprehensive: abbreviate, fragment, capture everything. But "I'll clean this up later" almost never happens, because later is the next inspection cycle.

Rewrite removes the trade-off. Microsoft confirms "Rewrite Page" as one of the four primary functions alongside Summarize, Create Task List, and Take Notes. The distinction from summarizing matters: **summarize** condenses, removing detail for clarity; **rewrite** transforms, reconstructing the same content with better structure and clearer language without necessarily shortening it. The detail stays; the rough edges go.

At City of Bowie, rewrite is most valuable for notes that become real documentation: a field inspection report going into the project file, a daily operations log feeding the quarterly report, a safety observation that must read cleanly for the record, a code compliance finding that a supervisor will review and sign off on.

:::{tip}
**Effective Rewrite Prompts for City of Bowie Staff**

- *"Rewrite this page as a formal field inspection report. Complete sentences, no abbreviations. Organize by: Site Access and Conditions, Infrastructure Findings, Code Compliance Observations, Safety Concerns, and Open Items."*
- *"Rewrite these dictated notes into a clean daily operations log entry for Parks & Recreation. Keep every location name, time, and work order number exactly as stated."*
- *"Rewrite this section as a code compliance site visit record suitable for the project file. Neutral, factual language. No speculation about cause."*
- *"Rewrite these project review notes into clear direction for the contractor. Separate what was approved from what is still open."*
:::

Each prompt gives Copilot a destination — not "better" but specifically what better means here.

### City of Bowie Application: Dictated Notes to Formal Record

A Parks & Recreation maintenance supervisor finishes day two of setup for a community event at Allen Pond Park. His notes are ninety seconds of dictation captured while walking the site, plus four photos: a vendor tent set up outside the permitted footprint, a power line run across a pedestrian path without proper covering, a restroom facility showing signs of a plumbing issue, and a contracted crew working on the north pavilion without a current City of Bowie vendor permit on file.

He prompts: *"Rewrite this page as a formal site setup log for Day 2 of the Allen Pond Park community event. Sections: Setup Progress by Zone, Utility and Power Concerns, Vendor and Facility Issues, Contractor and Permit Items, Safety Observations. Keep all location names, times, and permit numbers exactly as I said them. Flag anything that needs a decision before the event opens tomorrow."*

Two minutes of walking becomes the day's official log — read by the Parks Director, and source material for the post-event report six weeks later.

:::{admonition} Stewardship Check
:class: note

**Stewardship: Protect City resources, public trust, and the integrity of our work.**

Rewrite makes your notes look professional. It does not make them true. Copilot will faithfully polish a wrong permit number into a beautifully formatted wrong permit number. Read the output against the facts before it becomes the record — especially where names, times, measurements, and code references are involved.
:::

## 6. Capability 3 — Task Lists, Punch Lists, and Open Items

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-action-items-generation.png
:label: fig-ch10-action-items
:alt: Diagram showing Copilot in OneNote extracting action items from field inspection notes — left panel shows a dense OneNote page with infrastructure inspection notes containing mentions of follow-ups and commitments, center shows the Copilot sparkle icon with an arrow labeled 'Create Task List', right panel shows a clean checklist with specific action items including 'Confirm contractor corrective action for drainage structure', 'Repair damaged park facility path segment', 'Chase vendor certificate of insurance', 'Reconfirm utility marking with Miss Utility', with labels showing 'Source: Field Inspection Notes Page' and 'Output: Action Items with Owners'
:width: 80%
:align: center

Copilot extracts commitments buried in running notes and organizes them into structured task lists with owners, deadlines, and priorities. In city operations, this is the difference between an infrastructure punch list and a memory.
:::

Every pre-project meeting, every site visit, every daily operations shift ends with commitments: things someone promised to do, questions that need answers, items that must be fixed before a project milestone or a public event. Uncaptured, they evaporate — and in city government with fixed permit deadlines and resident-facing deliverables, evaporation shows up as a code violation, a missed deadline, or a constituent complaint.

Microsoft confirms Copilot in OneNote can create task lists from your notes, a page, or an entire section, incorporating deadlines and priorities when they appear in the source. Of the four capabilities this has the most direct effect on execution. Open Copilot on your notes, ask it to extract the action items, and copy the structured list into Microsoft Tasks, the project's Teams channel, or an infrastructure punch list section of the project notebook.

Specificity in, specificity out. "Talk to contractor about the drainage issue" produces a task that says talk to contractor about the drainage issue. "Jones Contracting to confirm by Thursday whether the inlet at Station 10+50 can be lined in place without excavation" produces a task you can close. Prompts worth keeping:

- *"Create a task list from this page. For each item note the responsible party where stated, the deadline, and the location or work order number it affects."*
- *"Extract all open items and split them into two lists: things the City owns, and things we are waiting on from the contractor, a utility company, another department, or a resident."*
- *"Create a prioritized infrastructure punch list from this section. Rank by what must be closed before the project milestone versus what can wait until the next inspection cycle."*

That second split — what we own versus what we are waiting on — is the most useful structure in public works project management. It separates what is in your control from what needs a phone call.

### City of Bowie Application: The Pre-Construction Meeting

A Public Works project manager finishes a pre-construction meeting with the contractor, a utility representative, and the City's field inspector: ninety minutes covering the mobilization sequence, revised work windows for two residential segments, a change to the traffic control plan, a late submission of erosion and sediment control documentation, and a contractor request to modify the project staging area. Commitments landed on all sides, captured in conversational flow — the order least useful afterwards.

She prompts: *"Create a task list from this page. Organize as: (1) City of Bowie actions with owner and due date, (2) Items we are waiting on from the contractor, (3) Items we are waiting on from the utility company or other parties. Note anything with a date inside the next five business days."*

The output goes into the project notebook's Open Items section and the Teams channel. When she runs the follow-up call ten days later, sections two and three are the agenda — already written.

## 7. Capability 4 — Drafting New Content from Your Notes

The fourth capability goes past transforming what exists: Copilot can **draft new content** grounded in your notebook. Microsoft's documentation confirms this includes event plans, presentation outlines, meeting agendas and drafts with discussion points and objectives, brainstormed ideas, and new pages and sections.

This is where OneNote stops being a capture tool and becomes a generation platform. Ask Copilot to draft next week's pre-construction meeting agenda from a section of site visit and contractor call notes, and it reflects the actual open items rather than a generic template. Ask it to brainstorm solutions connecting a resident's service request to the physical constraints you captured on the facility walkthrough. Ask it to outline a team session on what keeps going wrong during infrastructure closeout, from four projects' worth of lessons-learned notes.

### Using Chat for Analytical Requests

Copilot in OneNote also supports **open chat with intelligent responses** in the pane — follow-ups, refinements, exploration. That matters for analytical rather than mechanical questions:

*"Across the notes in this section from the last four inspections at this park, what problems have recurred? Group by theme and tell me which pages you drew from."*

*"I have a year of code compliance inspection notes in this section. What violations come up most often related to contractor signage and safety? Rank by frequency."*

*"Based on the field logs in this notebook, what consistently goes wrong in the first week of a capital project, and what did we do that worked?"*

These require reasoning across your content, not just condensing it. The chat interface exists for exactly this — and, as the next sections show, this is where the operational knowledge payoff actually lands.

## 8. The Facility Notebook — Where Institutional Memory Actually Lives

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-venue-notebook.png
:label: fig-ch10-venue-notebook
:alt: Illustration of a OneNote Facility Notebook for a City of Bowie park — notebook sidebar shows sections for Facility Profile, Infrastructure and Utilities, Maintenance History, Inspection History, and Lessons Learned — main page shows a structured Infrastructure and Utilities page with drainage structure inventory, pavement conditions, and a status table with green checkmarks, and a Copilot summary panel showing recurring issues across past inspection cycles
:width: 80%
:align: center

The Facility Notebook is the highest-value notebook in this chapter. Everything City of Bowie employees have learned about a park, a public building, or an infrastructure corridor — drainage quirks, contractor access constraints, utility conflicts — accumulated across every project and inspection cycle, and made searchable and summarizable by Copilot.
:::

If you build only one notebook after reading this chapter, build this one.

City of Bowie manages **16+ square miles** of territory, dozens of parks and recreation facilities, hundreds of lane miles of streets, a network of stormwater infrastructure, and numerous public buildings. The same facilities come up for inspection and maintenance year after year. The employees do not always stay the same. A Public Works inspector rotates to another assignment, a Parks maintenance supervisor retires, a code compliance officer transfers — and every time, hard-won, expensive, entirely undocumented knowledge about a specific site walks out with them.

That knowledge is specific: which drainage inlet backs up in a heavy rain event, which park pavilion has a weight limit nobody has posted officially, which utility company consistently delays marking in a particular corridor, which contractor access route is impassable after wet weather, where the underground infrastructure diverges from the GIS records, which facility contact actually returns calls.

None of that is in an official system. Most of it is in no system at all. It lives in the heads of people who happened to be there — until it does not. A facility notebook is the fix, and Copilot is what makes it worth keeping, because a notebook nobody can search is just a slower filing cabinet.

### Recommended Facility Notebook Structure

**Facility Profile** — stable facts: location, ownership, key assets, exclusive service providers, in-house restrictions, key contacts with names and direct numbers.

**Infrastructure and Utilities** — drainage structure inventory, pavement conditions, utility crossing locations, capacity constraints, advance utility marking notes, material handling access routes and gate hours, and what actual site conditions look like versus what the drawings show. Photograph everything.

**Maintenance and Contractor History** — which contractors have worked here, access constraints they encountered, known site conditions that affect bid pricing, common punch list items at this facility.

**Inspection History** — one page per inspection cycle, dated, with the inspector's name. Where the raw field captures land. Do not clean them into oblivion; dated, specific, imperfect notes beat a polished summary that lost the detail.

**Lessons Learned** — one page per project or major maintenance cycle at this facility: what went wrong, what we changed, what we would do differently. This section pays for the entire notebook.

### What Copilot Does With It

Once three or four project cycles are in there, the notebook becomes something no individual could be:

- *"Summarize everything in the Infrastructure and Utilities section into a one-page brief for a Public Works inspector who has never worked at this facility."*
- *"Across the Lessons Learned section, what problems have occurred more than once at this facility? Group by theme and cite the pages."*
- *"Create a task list of everything in this notebook that should be verified on the next site inspection because it may have changed."*
- *"Based on the Maintenance and Contractor History section, draft a briefing note for the field crew covering the three things that most commonly get misunderstood at this site."*

That third prompt is quietly the most valuable in the chapter. Operational knowledge decays — facilities change, drainage infrastructure shifts, utility maps get updated. A notebook that surfaces its own staleness stays trustworthy.

:::{admonition} Responsiveness Check
:class: note

**Responsiveness: Respond to resident and community needs with urgency and care.**

A facility notebook is written for the colleague who comes after you and will never get to ask you a question. Write it honestly. Record the thing that went wrong on your project, including the part that was the City's fault. A lessons-learned section full of tidy successes is worse than no section at all, because it teaches the next team nothing while looking like it does.
:::

## 9. The Project Notebook — One Cycle, Start to Finish

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-show-notebook.png
:label: fig-ch10-show-notebook
:alt: Illustration of a OneNote Project Notebook for a City of Bowie capital project cycle — notebook sidebar shows sections for Project Overview, Pre-Construction Meetings, Site Inspections, Daily Operations Log, Resident and Constituent Issues, Safety Walks, and Closeout and Lessons Learned — the main page shows a Daily Operations Log entry with location-by-location progress, contractor variance notes, an issue table and a Copilot-generated punch list on the right side
:width: 80%
:align: center

A Project Notebook carries one project cycle from kickoff through closeout — pre-construction meetings, site inspections, daily logs, constituent issues, safety observations, and the lessons that feed the next project phase.
:::

Where the facility notebook is permanent, the project notebook is a cycle: it opens at kickoff, closes with the project closeout report, and becomes the reference for the next maintenance or capital cycle.

**Project Overview** — dates, location, funding source, scope of City services, key contacts, and headline numbers: linear footage, affected parcels, department assignments.

**Pre-Construction and Planning Meetings** — one page per meeting, dated. Where the Copilot meeting recaps from Chapter 9 get filed so they live somewhere durable instead of scrolling out of a Teams channel.

**Site Inspections** — field findings specific to this project, including anything unusual in the contractor's work plan or the permit conditions. Facility-general findings get promoted into the facility notebook; project-specific findings stay here.

**Daily Operations Log** — one page per day. The most valuable section during active construction and the one most likely to be skipped. Dictate it on a phone at the end of each shift, then rewrite it with Copilot in ninety seconds.

**Resident and Constituent Issues** — one page per escalation: resident name or address, issue raised, what was communicated, what action was taken, how it closed. Your record when a Council member asks, and your evidence when a complaint is disputed.

**Safety Walks** — dated observations with photos, factual and neutral. Safety performance is a City operating priority, and observations that exist only in memory contribute nothing to it.

**Closeout and Lessons Learned** — project completion notes, punch list resolution, warranty items, and the candid retrospective.

Copilot prompts that earn their keep here:

- *"Summarize the Daily Operations Log section into a chronological narrative of how the project actually ran versus the schedule."*
- *"From the Resident and Constituent Issues section, group the escalations by root cause and tell me which were within the City's control."*
- *"Draft a project lessons-learned outline from this notebook. Separate what worked, what failed, and what we would change for the next project phase."*
- *"Create a task list of everything in this notebook that must be resolved before final project closeout."*

That last one is the difference between closeout as a reconstruction exercise and closeout as a review.

## 10. The Program Notebook and the Policy Ideation Notebook

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-account-notebook.png
:label: fig-ch10-client-discovery
:alt: Illustration of a structured OneNote Program Notebook for a City of Bowie department manager — notebook sidebar shows sections for Program Profile, Key Contacts, Annual History, Meeting Notes, Open Questions, and Next Steps, with the main page showing a Program History page listing multiple fiscal years with dates, scope, results and issues, and a Copilot summary panel at the bottom of the page
:width: 80%
:align: center

A Program Notebook holds the full arc of a departmental program across fiscal years and project cycles — profile, contacts, history, conversations, and open items — so that program knowledge survives a change in staff or administration.
:::

### The Program Notebook

For department managers and program coordinators, this is the program's memory — the facility notebook principle applied to services and people rather than physical infrastructure.

**Program Profile** — what the program does, what residents it serves, objectives, funding sources, how the City measures success, and which departments share responsibility.

**Key Contacts** — one page per stakeholder: name, title, decision authority, communication style, history with the department, and the human details that make a working relationship effective.

**Annual History** — one page per fiscal year: scope, key outcomes, what went wrong, how it was resolved, what residents said afterwards. Over three or four years this becomes the most valuable page set in the program.

**Meeting Notes**, **Open Questions**, and **Next Steps** round it out — dated pages for every call and briefing, what we still need to learn or have asked for and not received, and the current active list refreshed from Copilot's task extraction.

The prompt that makes this notebook indispensable, used when someone inherits a program: *"Summarize this notebook. Give me the program's objectives, the history of our work across fiscal years, the recurring issues, the open commitments, and the three things I most need to know before my first Council presentation."* Ninety seconds, and a new program manager walks into a meeting informed rather than apologetic.

### The Policy and Planning Ideation Notebook

Policy and planning work has a different shape. Ideas arrive out of order, half-formed, and their value is often not obvious until months later on a different initiative.

A planner's or administrator's ideation notebook is deliberately looser: **Concepts** (one page per idea, photos of comparable projects in other jurisdictions, reference materials), **Community Feedback** (resident input by topic and district), **Constraints and Context** (what was proposed versus what was adopted, and why), and **Research and Precedent Notes** (what worked in comparable Maryland municipalities, what failed and why).

Copilot's value here is not summarizing. It is retrieval and pattern-finding across a year of scattered thinking:

- *"Across the Concepts section, which ideas involved increasing park accessibility? Summarize each in two lines."*
- *"Based on this resident feedback and the constraints captured from the Planning Commission meeting, brainstorm five approaches to the Allen Pond Park expansion that respect the budget ceiling and the wetland buffer requirements."*
- *"From the Constraints and Context section, what has repeatedly caused a planning proposal to change between initial draft and final adoption?"*

That last question is hard to answer from memory and valuable to answer with evidence.

## 11. Team Notebooks — Shared Knowledge in Teams and SharePoint

:label: fig-ch10-teams-sharepoint
:alt: Integration diagram showing OneNote connected to Microsoft Teams and SharePoint — three icons arranged in a triangle with Teams top left, OneNote top right, and SharePoint bottom center, with bidirectional arrows between all three labeled 'Teams meeting recap to OneNote', 'Project notebook stored in SharePoint', and 'SharePoint department documents accessible from OneNote', with a central label reading 'Shared Knowledge Layer' and city department collaboration context

Teams, OneNote, and SharePoint form a triangular knowledge ecosystem — Teams generates the conversation, OneNote captures and organizes it, SharePoint stores and shares it. A project notebook that lives in SharePoint is available to the whole project team, across every City department involved.

Individual notebooks are useful. Shared notebooks — stored in SharePoint, tied to a Teams team — turn a distributed city department into a single knowledge organism. In July 2025, according to Schneider IT's tracking of M365 enterprise deployments, Copilot Notebooks gained real-time collaboration: multiple contributors working simultaneously with structure and annotations preserved.

Three earn their keep at City of Bowie. **Project team notebooks** — the project notebook from Section 9, shared, where the field inspector captures the daily log, the resident services coordinator logs constituent escalations, the project manager records contractor variance notes, and the department director files pre-construction recaps. Everyone works one record instead of five. **Facility and regional notebooks** — facility notebooks shared across the Public Works and Parks departments that maintain them, so staff who rotate assignments inherit each other's field knowledge. **Onboarding notebooks** — role guides, process documentation, City policies, accumulated practice, where a new code compliance officer or permit technician can ask Copilot to summarize key processes or draft a 30-day learning plan instead of consuming a senior colleague's workday with questions.

### The Curation Imperative

Schneider IT's documentation flags the risk directly: *"if a notebook omits crucial documents, Copilot can produce confident but incomplete answers — teams must curate notebooks."*

That is a property of every knowledge system, not a flaw. A maintained notebook produces reliable outputs. A stale one produces confident nonsense — worse than nothing, because it looks authoritative.

So someone must own each shared notebook: the project manager for a project notebook, the Parks superintendent for a facility notebook. Ownership means ensuring key findings get captured, promoting project-specific learnings into the facility notebook where they generalize, and marking outdated information as historical rather than leaving it to mislead someone in 2028.

## 12. The Note-Taking Discipline

:::{figure} ../images/ch10-note-taking-discipline.png
:label: fig-ch10-discipline
:alt: Two-column infographic showing 'Notes AI Can Work With' on the left — clear headers, consistent structure, named people, specific dates, location identifiers, explicit commitments, numbered action items — versus 'Notes AI Struggles With' on the right — stream of consciousness text, abbreviations without context, no names or dates, jumbled topics — with a quality spectrum bar at the bottom running from red to green and the caption 'Better notes equal better Copilot output'
:width: 80%
:align: center

Good notes for AI are not more bureaucratic notes — they are notes with names, numbers, dates, and explicit commitments. Structure is what lets Copilot produce something you can act on.
:::

Copilot's output is bounded by the quality of the notes. Here is what that means concretely in the field.

**Numbers, not adjectives.** "Inlet is blocked" is unusable. "Inlet at Station 10+50 on Northview Drive is 60% blocked with debris; clear span reduced from 36" to approximately 14"" is a note that informs a repair decision. Measurements, dimensions, percentages, times, address ranges, work order numbers — capture the number.

**Name people.** "The contractor's supervisor said" loses meaning in a week. "James Wheeler, site supervisor, Jones Contracting" keeps it — and makes the note searchable, which is how it gets reused.

**State commitments explicitly.** "Contractor to submit revised traffic control plan to City Engineering by Thursday COB." Implicit commitments buried in narrative are easy for Copilot — and for you — to miss.

**Timestamp field entries.** During active construction or a multi-day event setup, sequence is everything. A log entry recording when something was observed is evidence. One without is an opinion.

**Context at the top, headers in the middle.** Date, project name, location, who was present, why you were there — Copilot uses this to frame everything below, and so will the colleague reading it next year. Then break the body by topic: drainage, pavement, utilities, resident concerns, so you can summarize one thread for one audience.

None of this takes meaningfully more time. It takes writing the number instead of the impression, the name instead of the role, and the commitment instead of the gist.

## 13. What Copilot in OneNote Cannot Do

:::{warning}
**Verified Limitations of Copilot in OneNote**

These are confirmed constraints based on Microsoft's documentation and known platform behavior as of early 2026, and are subject to change.
:::

**It cannot access information outside the notebook context.** Unlike Copilot in Teams or Outlook, it works within the open page and notebook. It will not pull a site plan from SharePoint, check your calendar, or read a contractor's email thread unless that content is in your notebook.

**It cannot be explicitly directed to specific sections or notebooks by name** (as of December 2025). Per SuperSimple365's tracking of Microsoft's December 2025 UI update, users can no longer refer to "this section" or "this notebook" in prompts the way they previously could. Copilot works in the active page context — so organize so that what it needs is on the page you are working from.

**It cannot guarantee accuracy.** Copilot synthesizes; it does not verify. If your notes say a storm drain inlet is 24 inches and it is 18 inches, Copilot will faithfully repeat the error into a document that looks official. Source citations exist to help you check. The checking is yours.

**It is not available if Windows Information Protection (WIP) is enabled.** A Microsoft-confirmed constraint on some managed devices. Contact IT.

**It does not replace judgment about what to capture.** It cannot tell you that you should have photographed the utility crossing, or that the offhand comment from the contractor's foreman was the most important thing said all morning. That judgment is the professional skill; Copilot is downstream of it.

**Local-only notebooks are not fully supported.** Notebooks not synced to OneDrive or SharePoint may have limited or no Copilot functionality — and, for field staff on mobile devices, will not be accessible when needed.

## 14. Beyond the Notebook — How Copilot Cowork Extends This Work

Everything so far happens inside OneNote. But the most valuable thing about a project notebook is what gets *made* from it afterwards — and that is where **Microsoft Copilot Cowork**, generally available worldwide since June 16, 2026, changes the economics.

Copilot Chat is a conversation: one prompt, one response, you steer each step. Cowork is an **assignment**. You describe an outcome; it plans and executes multi-step work across Microsoft 365 and returns finished artifacts — documents, presentations, spreadsheets, emails, Teams posts.

Two properties matter here. Cowork browses your Work IQ — SharePoint and OneDrive folders, files, emails, Teams content — assembling across sources, which is exactly the boundary Copilot in OneNote alone cannot cross. And it **keeps working when your laptop is off**, in a protected cloud environment. For someone who assigns a task at the end of an inspection day and reviews it on a phone the next morning, that is the entire point.

### A City of Bowie Example: Project Notebook to Finished Deliverables

A capital project reaches substantial completion on a Thursday. The project manager's notebook holds four weeks of daily field logs, eleven resident issue pages, three safety walk records, the pre-construction meeting recaps, and a final punch list walkthrough page written at end of day. She opens Cowork and assigns this:

> **Outcome:** A project closeout lessons-learned document and a Council-facing project summary, both ready for my review by Monday morning.
>
> **Inputs:** The project notebook for this project, the pre-construction meeting recaps in the project's Teams channel, the contractor pay application workbook in the project SharePoint folder, and the facility notebook for this corridor.
>
> **Definition of done:** (1) An internal lessons-learned Word document — what worked, what failed, root cause, and recommendation for the next capital cycle — saved to the project folder. (2) A Council-facing project summary in PowerPoint, six slides maximum, covering scope delivered, key metrics, and how resident issues were resolved. (3) A draft email to my director summarizing the three things I want changed for the next project.
>
> **Constraints:** Do not contact the contractor or any resident. Keep all internal budget variance information out of the Council-facing deck. Use neutral, factual language for anything relating to safety or incidents.
>
> **Approval scope:** Show me the email before it is sent. Do not send anything externally under any circumstances.

Cowork works through it — reading the notebook, pulling the workbook, cross-referencing the facility notebook for recurring issues — and pauses for approval before the email. She reviews on Monday, corrects two attributions, approves, and the lessons-learned document lands in the facility notebook where the next project team will find it. That is the whole chapter in one motion: field capture on a phone, structured by Copilot, assembled by Cowork, filed where institutional memory lives.

Note the shape of the request. It follows Cowork's five-part pattern: **Outcome, Inputs, Definition of done, Constraints, Approval scope.** Vague assignments produce vague artifacts. This is the core skill, and Chapter 14 covers it in full.

:::{warning}
**Approve like your name is on it — because it is.**

Cowork asks permission before sensitive actions, and every task runs with your permissions, sees only what you can see, and is auditable. But a Council-facing summary deck built from your notebook is indistinguishable from one you wrote. If the notebook contained an error, the deck contains the error, and Council members read it as the City's official position. Cowork removes the assembly. It does not remove the responsibility.
:::

## 15. Try This — Build a Facility Notebook

:::{note}
**Hands-On Exercise: The Facility Notebook**

**Step 1: Create the structure.** In OneNote, create a notebook named for a City facility you have worked on more than once — a park, a public building, an infrastructure corridor. Create five sections: Facility Profile, Infrastructure and Utilities, Maintenance and Contractor History, Inspection History, Lessons Learned. Save it to SharePoint or OneDrive — not locally.

**Step 2: Empty your head into it.** Take twenty minutes and write down everything you personally know about this facility that is not written down anywhere official. Drainage quirks, access constraints, contractor lessons learned, the contact who actually responds to requests, the condition issue that keeps recurring. Do not polish it. Use the discipline principles from Section 12: numbers, names, explicit statements.

**Step 3: Summarize it.** Open the Copilot pane (Home → Copilot). Prompt: *"Summarize this page into a briefing for a City of Bowie employee who has never worked at this facility. Organize by: Access and Infrastructure, Maintenance Patterns, Known Constraints, and Things That Commonly Surprise People."* Review the output and check the citations. Where the summary is thin, your notes were thin.

**Step 4: Generate a verification list.** Prompt: *"Create a task list of everything in these notes that should be verified on the next site inspection because it may have changed or because I am not certain of it."* This becomes your inspection checklist — honest about what you know versus what you assume.

**Step 5: Share it.** Move the notebook into your team's SharePoint site or Teams team. Tell one colleague it exists and ask them to add one thing they know that you did not.

**Reflection:** Notice how much of what you wrote in Step 2 existed nowhere else. That is the size of the risk the City carries every time someone changes roles or retires. Then notice how long Step 2 actually took. That asymmetry — between the value of the knowledge and the cost of capturing it — is the argument for this entire chapter.
:::

## Glossary

```{glossary}
Copilot Pane
  The sidebar panel that opens on the right side of OneNote via the Home tab. The primary interface for entering prompts, receiving responses, and copying output.

Summarize (OneNote)
  A confirmed capability that generates structured summaries of a page, a selection, or a topic, with cited sources indicating which parts of the notes were used.

Create Task List
  A confirmed capability that extracts action items and commitments from notes into a structured list, with deadlines and priorities where noted.

Rewrite Page
  A confirmed capability that transforms rough or informal notes into clearer, better-organized documentation without necessarily reducing length or detail.

Source Citations
  Copilot's identification of which parts of your notes a summary drew from, enabling verification of the synthesis.

Team Notebook
  A notebook stored in SharePoint or associated with a Teams team, accessible to all members. Supports real-time collaboration as of Microsoft's 2025 updates.

Windows Information Protection (WIP)
  A Windows security feature that, when enabled on a managed device, prevents Copilot in OneNote from functioning.

Facility Notebook
  A notebook holding everything City of Bowie employees have learned about a specific park, public building, or infrastructure corridor — profile, infrastructure and utilities, maintenance history, inspection history, and lessons learned — accumulated across every project and maintenance cycle at that site.

Project Notebook
  A notebook covering one project cycle from kickoff to closeout: pre-construction meeting recaps, site inspections, daily operations logs, resident and constituent issues, safety walks, and project closeout lessons learned.

Program Notebook
  A notebook holding a departmental program across fiscal years — profile, key contacts, annual history, meeting notes, open questions, and next steps.

Policy Ideation Notebook
  A loosely structured notebook used by planners and administrators for policy concepts, community feedback, planning constraints, and research notes, with Copilot used for retrieval and pattern-finding across accumulated thinking.

Knowledge Capture Layer
  OneNote's role in the M365 ecosystem — between communication tools (Teams, Outlook) and storage (SharePoint, OneDrive), where field observations become structured, searchable records.

Note-Taking Discipline
  Capturing notes with specific numbers, named individuals, explicit commitments, timestamps, and clear structure — the prerequisite for high-quality Copilot output.

On-Canvas Icon
  The Copilot icon accessible directly from a page, giving quick access to Summarize, Create Task List, and Rewrite without using the ribbon.

Institutional Memory
  The accumulated operational knowledge of an organization — who knows what, how things work, what has been tried — that resides in people's experience and risks being lost when staff change roles or retire. OneNote notebooks, curated over time, are the mechanism for preserving it.
```

## Discussion

Before AI, a note's value was fixed at the moment of capture: what you wrote was what you had. With Copilot, that value is dynamic — every note is potential raw material for summaries, infrastructure punch lists, briefings, and pattern analysis generated on demand.

That shift raises a question worth working through as a team: **if a well-structured set of notes is now an asset the whole City can query, what should we be capturing that we currently are not?**

1. Think about a park, facility, or infrastructure corridor you know well. What do you personally know about it that exists nowhere in writing? What happens to that knowledge when you change assignments or retire?
2. Where has the City clearly lost operational knowledge between project cycles, and what would have prevented it?

3. Most field staff are not at a desk during their most information-rich moments. What would a capture habit have to look like to survive a full day of site visits? Be specific about the tools and the moments.
4. Who should own a shared facility or project notebook, and what does "owning" it require week to week?
5. Where is the line between a notebook that is useful and one that creates risk — notes about resident complaints, contractor performance, or internal budget discussions?

:::{admonition} Discussion Guidelines
:class: tip

Write a substantive initial response drawing on your actual work at City of Bowie, not hypotheticals. Reference at least one credible source — Microsoft's documentation, research on organizational knowledge management, or public sector reporting — to support a claim you make.

Then reply substantively to at least **two peers**. Not agreement — extension: a perspective they did not consider, an assumption worth challenging, or a contrasting example from your department or role.
:::

## Chapter Summary and Leader's Takeaway

OneNote has always been where knowledge goes to be organized. Copilot is what finally makes that organized knowledge work for you.

**Summarize**, with its source citations, turns a two-hour field inspection into a verified debrief before you leave the site. **Create Task List** pulls commitments out of a dictated field log into an infrastructure punch list with owners. **Rewrite** takes ninety seconds of talking into your phone at a park maintenance site and produces documentation that belongs in the project file. **Draft New Content** turns accumulated notes into the agenda, the briefing, the constituent update, and the lessons-learned outline that comes next. Cowork extends the chain further, assembling a whole notebook into finished deliverables while you are at the next inspection.

The notebook structures here — Facility, Project, Program, Policy Ideation — are not arbitrary. They map to where City of Bowie actually loses knowledge: between project cycles, between departments, and between the person who learned something and the person who needed to know it.

::::{important}
**Leader's Takeaway**

City of Bowie has served Prince George's County's largest community for over sixty years, and the institutional knowledge accumulated across those decades — about our parks, our streets, our infrastructure, and our residents — is one of the most valuable assets we have.

Right now that knowledge is mostly in people's heads. It is real, it is irreplaceable, and it leaves when they do. Across hundreds of projects, dozens of facilities, and thousands of resident interactions a year, that leakage is continuous and almost entirely invisible until a resident files a complaint or a Council member asks a question nobody can answer.

The people on your team who build good capture habits are not merely more organized. They are converting personal expertise into City capability. Your job is to make that conversion normal: give shared notebooks an owner, ask for the field inspection to land in the facility notebook rather than an inbox, treat the lessons-learned page as a deliverable rather than a courtesy, and read what your team writes so they know it is read.

Do that for four project cycles and the next inspector or coordinator who works a facility we have managed for twenty years will walk in knowing what we know. That is the whole point.
