> *"The blank page is the enemy. Copilot kills the blank page."*

Here is a scene that plays out across City Hall dozens of times every week.

A Public Works project manager has just finished a two-day field assessment of a neighborhood infrastructure corridor where the City will coordinate a major road rehabilitation project. She has photos on her phone, an engineering survey on her laptop, a page of scribbled notes about contractor staging areas and traffic signal timing, and the kind of mental clarity that only comes from physically walking a project site — knowing exactly where the utility conflict will be, which intersections will choke during construction, and which permit conditions require special coordination with the State Highway Administration.

She sits down at her desk, opens Microsoft Word, and stares at a blank white document.

Her task: write the project operations manual. Construction sequencing, traffic management windows, contractor staging assignments, safety protocols, resident notification schedules, escalation contacts. Clear, precise, operational. Something a crew of contractors across three phases can execute against without calling her at midnight. Something the City Administrator can read and feel confident about presenting to the City Council.

She knows exactly what needs to be said. She knows the corridor, the contractors, the resident concerns, the permit conditions. She just needs to say it — and saying it properly, in the right structure, at the right length, in language every party will read the same way, will take her the better part of a day. Because that's how long it has always taken.

Now she opens Copilot in Word.

She describes what she needs in a prompt. She references the field assessment notes and the previous project's operations manual, both already saved in SharePoint. Copilot produces a structured first draft in under a minute. Not a finished manual — a serious starting point. The structure is right. The sections are labeled. The register is operational and unambiguous. Now she is not writing from a blank page. She is editing from a solid foundation.

Her eight-hour task becomes ninety minutes of focused revision and verification — and the ninety minutes she spends are spent on the parts only she can do: the construction sequencing judgment, the utility conflict nuance, the note about the contractor staging area that creates a sight-line issue near an elementary school crosswalk.

**This chapter is about that shift.** It is about how Copilot in Word works — based on Microsoft's own documented capabilities — what it can and cannot do, and how City of Bowie employees apply it to the documents that define our work: constituent service guides, department SOPs, grant applications, inter-agency proposals, vendor scopes of work, after-action reports, site assessment writeups, incident reports, budget narratives, permit packets, project closeout reports, and the standard operating procedures that keep City departments serving Bowie's 70,000+ residents with accountability, responsiveness, stewardship, and pride.

There is one rule that Microsoft itself has built into the product's design — and that we will return to throughout this chapter: **every draft Copilot generates requires your verification before it leaves your hands.** Microsoft states this explicitly in their own documentation: *"Remember that Copilot generates a draft. You'll need to verify and modify details to make sure it's accurate and fits your tone and style."* Not because Copilot is unreliable — it is genuinely impressive. Because the professional who sends the document is responsible for it. In a City government, a document that is wrong does not produce a polite correction email. It produces a contractor in the wrong location at the wrong time with the wrong instructions, a resident who received incorrect information about a permit deadline, or a grant application that fails review because a compliance requirement was mischaracterized.

No AI changes that. Let's start with the three capabilities.

## 1. The Three Capabilities of Copilot in Word

Copilot in Word is not a single button. It is three distinct capabilities, each designed for a different moment in the document lifecycle. Understanding which one to use — and when — is the first competency to develop.

Add to Existing Document when your draft is underway and needs more content.

### Capability 1: Start a Draft

This is the blank-page killer. When you open a new Word document — or click the Copilot icon in your document — you will see an option to draft with Copilot. Type a prompt describing what you need, and Copilot generates a full structured draft.

After Copilot produces its output, you have four choices: **Keep it**, **Regenerate** (ask Copilot to try again), **Discard** (start over), or **refine with a follow-up prompt** — for example, *"Make it more concise"* or *"Add a section on traffic management sequencing and contractor staging"* or *"Change the tone so a first-time permit applicant can follow it."* This iterative refinement loop is where Copilot becomes genuinely powerful: you do not have to get the perfect draft on the first try. You and Copilot work toward it together.

The critical insight about Start a Draft is that **prompt quality is the primary driver of output quality.** A thin prompt produces a generic draft. A rich prompt — one that includes the document's purpose, intended audience, key points to cover, required structure, and tone — produces a draft that requires substantially less revision.

Compare these two prompts for the same document:

**Thin prompt:** *"Write a scope of work for a city construction project."*

**Rich prompt:** *"Write a formal Scope of Work document for a City of Bowie vendor engagement. Project: Kenilworth Avenue Corridor Rehabilitation — 1.4 miles of road resurfacing, curb and gutter replacement, and ADA ramp upgrades. City responsibilities oversight: Department of Public Works serving as project manager. Vendor responsibilities: full roadway resurfacing, milling, curb/gutter work, ADA ramp construction, traffic control plan implementation, and temporary signage. Construction phasing: three phases over 14 weeks. Traffic management: no full road closures permitted; single-lane alternating traffic with flaggers. Excluded from vendor scope: utility work, stormwater modifications, and permanent signage, which the City provides through separate contractors. Structure the document with these sections: Project Overview, Scope of Services, Services Excluded, Construction Phasing, Traffic Management Requirements, Materials and Standards, Resident Communication Obligations, Reporting and Inspections, Assumptions and Dependencies. Formal contractual register suitable for a City procurement and Public Works review team."*

The thin prompt produces a template. The rich prompt produces a first draft that a Public Works director can actually work with. The difference is three minutes of additional prompt work — consistently the highest-leverage investment in Copilot productivity.

### Capability 2: Base a Draft on an Existing File, Email, or Meeting

This is where Copilot in Word becomes a different kind of tool entirely. Rather than generating content from a prompt alone, you can instruct Copilot to **ground its draft in specific source material you already have** — a SharePoint document, a OneDrive file, or a meeting transcript.

The mechanism is the **slash reference** (`/`). When composing your Copilot prompt in Word, type `/` and a file picker appears. You can reference up to 20 items in a single prompt — and those items become the factual foundation for what Copilot writes.

For example: *"Summarize the operational lessons from / [after-action report — prior construction season] and write a Risks and Mitigations section suitable for this year's project operations manual. Focus on contractor staging conflicts, traffic management compliance, and resident notification gaps."*

Copilot reads the referenced file, extracts the relevant content, and grounds its draft in what is actually in that document — not in what it generally knows about municipal construction management.

**The requirement:** Files must be saved in SharePoint or OneDrive to be referenceable. Local files saved only to your desktop or C: drive cannot be pulled into a Copilot prompt. If you are working with documents that live on a local drive — and in a field-heavy role, plenty of them do — save them to your OneDrive first. Do this at the project site, before you return to City Hall. Future-you, trying to draft the closeout report under a deadline, will be grateful.

This capability is particularly powerful for:

- Department SOPs drafted from actual field assessment notes and regulatory requirements
- Constituent service guides built from current fee schedules, permit requirements, and processing deadlines
- After-action reports grounded in actual project logs, inspection records, and contractor correspondence
- Grant applications drawn from prior year grant narratives, program guidelines, and the City's strategic priorities
- Inter-agency proposals grounded in the partner agency's actual request document rather than your memory of it
- SOP revisions based on the existing procedure document being updated

The output is not Copilot's general knowledge about what a City operations manual looks like. It is Copilot's synthesis of what is actually in your documents. That distinction matters enormously for accuracy — and in municipal government, accuracy is the whole job.

### Capability 3: Add Content to an Existing Document

The third capability addresses a different moment in the writing process: your document is already underway, and you need to add to it. Rather than starting over or switching contexts, you can position your cursor in the document, invoke Copilot, and ask it to add a new section, expand an existing section, or generate content that fits naturally with what is already written.

This is particularly useful for:

- Adding a Safety and Incident Reporting section to an operations manual that was started without one
- Drafting an executive summary for a long grant application after the narrative body is complete
- Adding a "What Happens Next" section to a resident notification letter
- Expanding a bullet-point punch list into full narrative paragraphs for a department director briefing
- Adding standard terms, deadline tables, or compliance language to a permit packet draft
- Appending a lessons-learned section to an after-action report once a project phase is complete

Copilot in this mode is context-aware: it reads what you have already written and matches the tone, structure, and register of your existing content. If your document is formal and regulatory, Copilot's additions will be formal and regulatory. If it is resident-facing and plain-spoken, additions will match that register.

### Example 1: The Grant Application Narrative

The grant application is the centerpiece document of many City departments' funding strategy. It is the formal narrative that supports every competitive award — the document that tells the project's story to the funding agency, describes the implementation plan, and demonstrates that the City of Bowie understands the program's priorities, the community's needs, and the operational capacity to execute successfully.

Writing a grant application from scratch is skilled work. It requires understanding the program requirements, knowing what the review panel needs to see, organizing complex project and budget information into a coherent narrative, and doing all of this in the register that a federal or state funding agency demands.

Copilot does not replace that skill. It eliminates the scaffolding problem — the time spent setting up the structure, writing the section headers, and producing the required boilerplate before the real work of strategy and judgment can begin.

**Prompting approach:**

Open a new Word document. Invoke Copilot (click the Copilot icon or select Draft with Copilot). If you have the grant program guidelines and the prior year application or project closeout report in SharePoint or OneDrive, reference them with `/`. Then use this prompt structure:

> *"Write a formal grant application narrative for the City of Bowie, Maryland. Base the project description on / [grant program guidelines] and / [prior project closeout report]. Funding program: [program name and agency]. Project: [project title and brief description]. City demographics: Bowie, Maryland — approximately 70,000+ residents, largest city in Prince George's County, incorporated 1963. Key community needs relevant to this program: [list]. City strengths and differentiators to emphasize: strong track record of project delivery, City Council oversight and accountability structures, partnerships with Prince George's County and State agencies, dedicated department staff with relevant expertise, and the City's commitment to Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, and Pride. Include these sections: Project Overview, Statement of Need and Community Context, Goals and Objectives, Proposed Approach and Implementation Plan, Organizational Capacity and Key Personnel, Evaluation and Reporting Plan, Budget Narrative and Cost Justification, Sustainability and Long-Term Impact. Professional, evidence-based register suitable for a federal or state grant review panel."*

Copilot produces a structured draft in under a minute. Your work then becomes:

- Verifying every figure — population data, project cost estimates, timeline milestones — against the actual program guidelines and City data
- Reviewing every factual claim about the City, the community need, and the project's alignment with program priorities
- Adding the strategic narrative that comes from your direct knowledge of the City's history with this funding agency and this type of project
- Adjusting the compliance and eligibility section to reflect the City's actual status and qualifications, which Copilot cannot know with certainty
- Confirming that every commitment made in the narrative is one the department can actually deliver
- Applying the five-point verification checklist before the application goes to the funding agency

The division of labor is clear: Copilot handles structure. You handle facts, judgment, and accountability.

::::{admonition} 🎯 City Values Check: Responsiveness
:class: note

**Responsiveness** — *we act promptly and meet our commitments to residents and partners.*

A grant application is a promise. Every sentence Copilot generates about project timelines, staffing levels, or deliverable milestones is a sentence the City will be held to when the grant award is made. Read the draft as the person who will have to execute it, not as the person trying to win the funding. If the department cannot deliver it on the stated schedule, it does not go in the application — no matter how well Copilot wrote the paragraph.

### Example 2: A Regulatory Compliance Operations Brief

Operations, planning, and public safety staff across City departments regularly produce briefing documents: summaries of a new state or county regulation, assessments of how updated compliance requirements apply to the City's specific practices, safety bulletins following an incident, or documentation responding to an agency inquiry. These documents require a specific structure and register — formal, precise, defensible, and grounded in the actual source text rather than in institutional memory.

Copilot handles this document type well when you reference the actual source material using `/`. Here is the recommended approach:

**Step 1:** Save the source document — the state agency's updated regulation, the county code revision, the federal compliance guidance memo, the inspection findings letter — to your OneDrive or a SharePoint folder you have access to.

**Step 2:** Invoke Copilot in a new Word document. Reference the saved document with `/`.

**Step 3:** Prompt:

> *"I am a department manager at the City of Bowie preparing a compliance update for department staff. Using the source document / [regulatory guidance document], write an internal operations brief suitable for department supervisors and front-line staff. Include these sections: (1) Summary — plain-language description of what the regulation requires; (2) Key Requirements — the specific obligations it creates for the City; (3) Applicability to Our Operating Practices — how these requirements map to how we currently run our programs and services; (4) Recommended Actions — what the department needs to change or confirm before the compliance deadline; (5) Timeline — realistic sequencing against the regulatory deadline. Formal operational register."*

**Step 4:** Review the draft critically, section by section:

- *Summary* — compare Copilot's characterization against the actual source text. Verify nothing was omitted or reframed in a way that changes meaning. A regulation's requirement either says what Copilot claims it says or it does not.
- *Applicability to Our Operating Practices* — Copilot will make reasonable assumptions about how City departments currently operate. Each assumption must be verified by someone who actually runs these programs and services.
- *Recommended Actions* — these require department leadership, legal, and where relevant risk management review before distribution. Copilot's suggestions are a starting point, not authoritative legal guidance. Regulatory interpretation is often context-dependent and nuanced — including an AI summary is no substitute for City Attorney review on substantive legal questions.

Compliance and regulatory briefs are high-stakes documents. Copilot gives you a professional structure and a strong first draft. Human expertise validates every substantive claim.

### Example 3: A Standard Operating Procedure Update

SOPs are among the most tedious documents to write — long, carefully structured, laden with defined terms and cross-references. They are also typically written by a single person working alone, which creates both efficiency problems (one person doing everything) and quality problems (one person self-editing without a second perspective).

And at the City of Bowie the stakes are real, because an SOP does not serve one location or one shift. It governs how departments serve Bowie's 70,000+ residents, coordinates with Prince George's County and State agencies, and defines the consistent standard of service that earns the City its reputation for accountability. A procedure that reads clearly in the Public Works yard and confusingly in Parks & Recreation is not a finished procedure.

Copilot in Word significantly accelerates SOP drafting when you reference the existing procedure using `/`. This grounds the revision in the actual current document rather than in Copilot's general sense of what a government procedure looks like.

> *"I am updating the City of Bowie's Constituent Service Request Intake SOP. Using the existing procedure in / [current constituent service SOP], revise and expand it to address the following additions: (1) a standardized intake documentation checklist to be applied at every service channel — phone, email, walk-in, and online portal; (2) a revised escalation and response workflow, including acknowledgment standards and a 48-hour status update requirement to residents; (3) an explicit section noting where departmental variation is permitted — service-type-specific intake requirements, department-specific response timelines, and coordinated responses with County or State agencies — so the procedure standardizes outcomes without overriding legitimate departmental operating realities. Maintain the existing document's formal register, numbering, and section structure. Effective date: [date]."*

The result is a procedure revision that builds on your actual existing document rather than starting from scratch — which means section numbers, defined terms, and cross-references stay consistent, and staff who already trained against version 4 are not handed a version 5 that reorganizes everything for no reason.

::::{admonition} 💡 The Five-Element Prompt Structure
:class: tip

Regardless of document type, the best prompts for Copilot in Word share five elements:

1. **Your role** — "I am a Public Works project manager / Finance analyst / HR coordinator / Planning staff member / Constituent Services supervisor at the City of Bowie"
2. **Document purpose** — What is this document for, and who will read it? A City Council briefing reads very differently from a resident permit guide.
3. **Source references** — Use `/` to reference up to 20 specific files already in SharePoint or OneDrive
4. **Required sections** — Name them explicitly; Copilot fills labeled sections more accurately than unlabeled ones
5. **Tone and register** — Formal and regulatory? Resident-facing and plain-spoken? Executive summary for City leadership? Operational and directive for field staff?

Memorize this pattern. Apply it every time. Your first drafts will consistently require less revision.
::::

### The 80-Page Regulatory Package Scenario

City of Bowie staff review an enormous volume of long, dense documents every year: state agency regulatory packages, federal program compliance guides, intergovernmental agreements, grant program requirements, county code amendments, construction and engineering specifications, environmental review documents, and the service manuals of programs the City is inheriting or transitioning. A regulatory compliance package from MDE or SHA can run 40, 60, even 80 pages. Reading one thoroughly — extracting applicable deadlines, reporting requirements, eligibility conditions, prohibited activities, inspection obligations, penalty provisions, and interagency coordination requirements — is a 90-minute minimum task for a trained professional.

Multiply that across dozens of active programs and projects, across seven departments serving 70,000+ residents, and you begin to see the scale of the opportunity.

Here is how to do it:

**Step 1:** If the package is a PDF, convert it to Word (File → Open → select the PDF — Word converts automatically). Save the converted document to SharePoint or OneDrive.

**Step 2:** Open a new Word document. Invoke Copilot.

**Step 3:** Reference the package with `/` and prompt:

> *"Summarize this regulatory package for a City of Bowie department team preparing for compliance. Base your summary on / [regulatory package]. Extract and organize: (1) Regulatory authority and program description; (2) Effective dates and compliance deadlines; (3) Key requirements — what the City must do; (4) Prohibited activities and restrictions; (5) Reporting and documentation obligations; (6) Inspection, audit, or certification requirements; (7) Interagency coordination requirements with county, state, or federal partners; (8) Penalty and corrective action provisions; (9) Resident or public notification obligations; (10) Material risk flags — anything unusual or operationally problematic for a municipal government. Format as a structured one-page compliance summary."*

Copilot processes the referenced document and produces the extraction. What you receive is a roadmap: a structured summary that surfaces the material provisions and flags potential risk areas for deeper investigation.

**The verification step — non-negotiable:** Review every extracted provision against the source document. Check the compliance deadlines against the actual regulatory text. Verify the reporting requirements against the agency's published forms and instructions. Confirm the eligibility and restriction provisions, because getting those wrong is the difference between a compliant program and an audit finding. Copilot's summarization is highly reliable on well-structured documents, but **numerical details — dates, dollar thresholds, submission windows, performance standards — must always be verified against the source.** A compliance plan built on a misread deadline is an error regardless of which tool produced it, and the regulatory agency will not be flexible about it.

### Summarization Beyond Regulatory Packages

The same workflow applies across the document types City of Bowie staff work with:

**Project closeout and after-action inputs saved in SharePoint:**
> *"Using / [project punch list and inspection logs] and / [contractor correspondence], identify trends in schedule adherence, cost variances, and unresolved punch list items. Flag any metrics that fall outside our planning assumptions for a project of this scope. Write an operational narrative suitable for inclusion in the project closeout report."*

**Grant program RFPs and NOFAs saved in OneDrive:**
> *"Using / [grant program NOFA], write a one-page summary of the eligibility requirements, the evaluation criteria and their weighting, and the submission deadlines. Identify the three requirements that will most affect our application narrative and budget."*

**Field assessment writeups:**
> *"Using / [field assessment notes and photo log], summarize the key findings. Identify any site conditions — access constraints, utility conflicts, ADA compliance gaps, drainage issues, or structural concerns — that would materially affect our project scope or timeline."*

**Incident and safety documentation:**
> *"Using / [incident report], produce a factual summary suitable for internal safety review: what happened, when, where on site, who was involved by role, immediate actions taken, and any corrective actions identified. Neutral, factual register. Do not speculate about cause beyond what the report states."*

**Prior meeting notes or call transcripts:**
> *"Using / [meeting notes — project kick-off with contractor], extract the key points relevant to the project operations manual: contractor commitments, changes to the construction sequence, revised deadlines, and open items requiring follow-up with owners."*

**Grant program guidelines for an application under development:**
> *"Using / [federal program guidelines] and / [City strategic plan], write a project narrative section describing how the proposed project aligns with both the funder's priorities and the City's long-term goals. Note any constraints the program guidelines impose on eligible activities, match requirements, or reporting."*

In every case, the Copilot output is grounded in your actual document — not in general knowledge — because you referenced it with `/`. That grounding is what makes summarization useful in a professional context where accuracy matters and compliance deadlines do not move.

## 4. Tone Adjustment — Same Content, Three Audiences

This is where Copilot in Word earns its highest praise from experienced City employees. The ability to take a single set of facts and express them in the right voice for three different audiences is a skill that separates good communicators from exceptional ones. Copilot accelerates this navigation dramatically — without replacing the professional judgment that makes it reliable.

One project, three audiences, three completely different documents. Copilot navigates the structural and tonal differences quickly. The professional's judgment ensures that each version is not just tonally appropriate but factually accurate and contextually complete.

### The Three-Audience Challenge

Consider a significant infrastructure project the City has just launched — a $2.4 million corridor rehabilitation project with a 14-week construction window, an intergovernmental agreement with Prince George's County, and direct impact on residents along a major arterial road. The same project needs to be described three different ways:

**For City leadership:** The Mayor, City Administrator, and City Council want strategic context. How does this project fit the City's Capital Improvement Plan? What does the budget and schedule profile look like? What is the risk to adjacent projects or department capacity? Does this project advance the City's goals for infrastructure stewardship and resident quality of life? They do not want to read the contractor's traffic control plan. They want to know what it means for the City and its residents.

**For the partner agency or grant funder:** The state transportation agency or federal program officer wants precision, documentation, and confidence. They want to see the implementation plan, the staffing model, the compliance commitments, the resident communication schedule, the escalation path, and the reporting cadence. They want to know that when residents call with questions during construction, somebody at the City has a plan for answering all of them. They want language that survives scrutiny in an intergovernmental review.

**For the resident:** The resident wants to know what is happening on their street and by when. Construction start dates. Traffic impacts. Parking restrictions. Where to call with questions. What the project will look like when it is done. They want confidence, directness, and plain language — not government jargon that makes a resident feel like they are reading a legal document instead of a neighbor's note.

That last audience deserves emphasis. The City of Bowie serves 70,000+ residents. A meaningful share of any communication reaches people with limited time, limited familiarity with government processes, or limited English proficiency. Every sentence of unnecessary jargon in a resident letter becomes a phone call to Constituent Services during the busiest phase of a project.

### Using Copilot to Navigate Tone

**Step 1:** Write — or have Copilot draft — a neutral factual summary of the project. All the facts, no audience-specific framing. Save it to OneDrive so you can reference it.

**Step 2:** Open a new document. Prompt Copilot for the first audience version, referencing the factual summary:

> *"Using / [project fact summary], write a one-paragraph description of this project for the City Administrator and City Council. Emphasize strategic fit with the Capital Improvement Plan, the resident impact and community benefit, and the key operational risks at a program level. Avoid granular construction detail. Formal but accessible register."*

**Step 3:** Repeat for each audience, each time referencing the same source document:

> *"Using / [project fact summary], write a project narrative for a state agency partner or grant funder's review package. Include project goals, implementation sequencing, key milestones, intergovernmental coordination plan, resident communication strategy, on-site staffing and escalation structure, and the reporting and closeout commitment. Precise, operationally confident register suitable for a program officer's review."*

> *"Using / [project fact summary], write a resident notification letter introducing this project. Tell residents what the City is doing, the three dates that will affect them most, what the construction will look like near their home, and who to contact with questions. Warm, plain-spoken, and direct. Assume the reader may have no prior knowledge of this project. No technical jargon without a plain-language explanation — if you use terms like utility relocation or traffic control plan, define them in one short sentence."*

**Step 4:** Review each version against its intended audience:

- The leadership version needs your judgment about what is strategically material right now, not what is generically true
- The partner/funder version needs independent verification of every operational number, date, and commitment
- The resident version needs your knowledge of what actually confuses residents at this phase of a project — Copilot can write clear language but cannot know that the intersection closure at this particular location has already generated three calls to the hotline

Copilot gets each version to 80–90% of where it needs to be. The final 10–20% is yours — and it is the part that makes the document genuinely excellent rather than merely competent.

## 5. One City, Seven Departments — Copilot as a Consistency Engine

There is a document problem that is specific to a city government's shape, and it is worth naming directly.

The City of Bowie produces the *same kinds of documents* over and over, in substantial volume, across seven departments. Constituent service guides. Department SOPs. Vendor scopes of work. Field assessment writeups. Budget narratives. After-action reports. Incident reports. Grant applications. Permit packets. Across thousands of resident interactions per year, dozens of capital projects, and seven departments serving a diverse community of 70,000+, many versions of these documents exist — written by different staff members, in different departments, at different times, in different levels of quality.

That is not a criticism of anyone. It is the natural consequence of a City that has grown from a 1963 incorporated town of a few thousand to a major Maryland municipality with a professional government staff, diverse resident needs, and an expanding portfolio of programs and services. Documents accumulate the way organizational complexity accumulates.

The problem it creates is real, though. When a resident interacts with the Parks & Recreation Department and then the Planning Department, they should recognize the City of Bowie's voice. When a grant funder reviews an application from Public Works and then Finance, both should feel like they came from the same professionally run organization. And when a new staff member joins in any department, the SOP they are handed should not be a document that only makes sense to the person who wrote it two years ago.

### What Copilot Actually Does Here

Copilot in Word is unusually good at this problem, for a simple reason: with the slash reference, you can point it at the version of a document you consider *right* and ask it to bring another version into line — without losing what is legitimately specific to a department or program.

**Pattern 1 — Standardize structure, preserve local content.**

> *"Using / [reference constituent service guide — approved template] as the structural model and / [current service guide for this program] as the content source, rewrite the current guide so it follows the approved structure, section order, and heading language exactly. Preserve every program-specific and department-specific detail from the content source: deadlines, fees, contact information, eligibility requirements, and process steps unique to this program. Do not standardize anything that is genuinely specific to this service. Where the content source is missing a section that the approved structure requires, insert the heading and mark it clearly as [TO BE COMPLETED] rather than inventing content."*

That last instruction matters more than it looks. Telling Copilot to flag gaps instead of filling them is the difference between a consistency tool and a fabrication engine.

**Pattern 2 — Harmonize terminology across departments.**

Terminology drift is a genuine operational risk in City communications. *Permit* and *authorization*. *Resident* and *constituent* and *citizen*. *Inspection* and *review* and *site visit*. Different departments use different words for the same thing, and some of that is correct and should stay. But within a single document set or program guide it should be deliberate.

> *"Review / [document] and produce a terminology consistency report: list every instance where the same service concept is described with different terms, and every instance where a government term is used without a plain-language definition on first use. Do not rewrite the document — just produce the list, with the section reference for each instance."*

**Pattern 3 — Bring an inherited document up to City of Bowie standard.**

When the City takes over a program from a county agency or a previous service provider, the inherited documentation is written in someone else's voice, structure, and assumptions.

> *"Using / [inherited program documentation] as the content source, rewrite it to match the structure and register of / [City of Bowie approved department template]. Retain all factual program and eligibility content from the inherited document. Flag with [VERIFY] any statement about services, eligibility criteria, or responsibilities that would need to be confirmed against the City's actual scope for this program rather than carried over from the previous provider."*

**Pattern 4 — Localize without fragmenting.**

> *"Using / [citywide SOP], produce a department-adapted version for our [department] team. Keep every requirement, numbered section, and defined term from the citywide SOP unchanged. Add a clearly labeled 'Department Application' subsection under each affected section describing how the requirement is executed given this department's specific programs, staff structure, and service channels. Do not delete or soften any citywide requirement. Mark clearly where departmental practice creates a conflict that needs escalation rather than resolving it yourself."*

### The Discipline Behind the Capability

None of these patterns work without one prerequisite: **somebody has to decide what "right" looks like, and that document has to live in SharePoint where Copilot can reach it.**

This is the unglamorous part. Copilot cannot standardize toward a template that only exists in one person's head or on one person's desktop. Building and maintaining a small, well-governed library of approved reference documents — the approved constituent service guide structure, the approved operations manual template, the approved SOP format, the approved after-action report shape — is what converts Copilot from a drafting assistant into a genuine consistency engine across the City.

::::{admonition} ⚠️ Consistency Is Not Sameness
:class: warning

There is a failure mode worth guarding against here. If you ask Copilot to make everything identical, it will happily make everything identical — including the things that are different for good reason.

Program eligibility requirements vary by funding source. Permit processes are service-type-specific and legally defined. Fee schedules differ by program and are set by City Council resolution. Reporting requirements differ significantly between federal, state, and locally funded programs. Resident notification obligations vary by project type and regulatory framework.

Always instruct Copilot explicitly on what must **not** be standardized. "Preserve all program-specific and funding-source-specific content exactly as written" belongs in every consistency prompt you run. A citywide-consistent document that is program-specifically wrong is worse than an inconsistent one that is program-specifically right.

## 6. Document Restructuring — Turning Bullets into Narrative

One of the most common writing tasks in a City employee's queue is the reformed bullet list — the email, the field note, the rushed project update written in bullets because that was the fastest way to capture the content, but that now needs to become a formal report, a resident memo, or a structured briefing.

Copilot in Word handles this conversion with impressive consistency.

:alt: Side-by-side comparison showing a bullet-point field note on the left labeled 'What You Have' — with rough, unstructured bullets about a project site condition — and a formatted professional department memo on the right labeled 'What Copilot Produces' — with three clearly labeled sections: Context, Recommendation, Next Steps — connected by an arrow through a Copilot icon in the center, professional City of Bowie brand colors, wide landscape format

Bullets capture thinking. Narrative communicates it. Copilot converts the capture format into the communication format — preserving all the content while adding the structure, transitions, and professional flow that formal City documents require.

### Why This Matters in City Government

City of Bowie staff are expert bullet-point writers. When you are managing a project site, responding to a constituent complaint, or coordinating with a contractor under deadline pressure, bullets are the only realistic capture format. They are fast, scannable, and great for internal handoffs.

But external documents — resident communications, grant reports, City Council briefings, inter-agency correspondence, after-action reports — require narrative flow. They require transitions, logical connectors, and a prose structure that guides the reader from context through analysis to conclusion.

The gap between "what the project manager knows" and "what the document says" is often a formatting gap, not an information gap. Copilot closes that gap in under a minute — and the Rewrite capability lets you refine the result with follow-up prompts until it lands exactly right.

This is also, bluntly, a quality-of-life improvement. The after-action report has historically been written by staff who have just finished the most intense phase of a project. Anything that shortens the distance between a bulleted punch list and a finished, professional document is time returned to people who have earned it.

### The Conversion Workflow

**Step 1:** Copy your bullet-point content. If it is from an email already in Outlook or SharePoint, save it to OneDrive and reference it with `/`.

**Step 2:** Invoke Copilot in a new Word document.

**Step 3:** Prompt:

> *"Convert the following bullet points into a formal, professional memo from the City of Bowie with three clearly labeled sections: (1) Context — a brief paragraph establishing the background and purpose of this communication; (2) Recommendation — a clear, direct statement of what the City is recommending and why; (3) Next Steps — a numbered list of specific actions with responsible parties and target dates. Professional tone in the City of Bowie official voice — clear, accessible, and accountable — suitable for sending directly to a department director or partner agency. Bullets: [paste bullets]"*

**Step 4:** Review the output. Use a follow-up refinement prompt if needed: *"Make the recommendation section more direct"* or *"The context section is too long — condense to two sentences."*

**Step 5:** Apply the verification checklist. Then read the final version from the recipient's perspective: does it answer their most important question, which is almost always *"What do you need from me and when?"*

### Rewrite — The Related Capability

The Rewrite capability in Copilot in Word is the specialized version of this for existing text. Select any passage in a document you have already written, invoke Copilot, and choose Rewrite. Copilot offers alternative versions of the selected text — and you can keep any one of them, ask it to try again, or combine elements from multiple suggestions.

Rewrite is the mode for:

- Making dense regulatory or code language readable for a resident who has never filed a permit
- Sharpening an executive summary in a grant application that is running too long
- Tightening an after-action narrative that has become repetitive
- Converting passive constructions to active voice throughout a procedure — critical in an SOP, where "the form will be submitted" is meaningfully worse than "the supervisor submits the form"
- Adjusting a single paragraph's register without rewriting the whole document

The important thing to understand about Rewrite is that it revises **within** the boundaries of the meaning you have already established. It is principled editing, not creative reinvention. The facts do not change. The structure does not change. The prose quality improves.

### The Restructuring Prompt Library

These prompts cover the most common restructuring tasks across City of Bowie departments:

**Pre-construction notes → formal project kickoff minutes:**
> *"Convert these project kickoff meeting notes into formal meeting minutes with sections for: Attendees and Roles, Key Decisions, Changes to the Project Plan, Action Items (with owner and due date), and Next Meeting date. Formal register suitable for the project file and for distribution to the contractor and department director."*

**Internal project update → executive briefing:**
> *"Convert this internal project update into a one-page executive briefing for the City Administrator or City Council. Lead with the key decision or finding, follow with supporting context, end with recommended action. Two paragraphs maximum. Executive audience."*

**Punch list → resident-facing status update:**
> *"Convert this construction punch list into a resident-facing project status update. Group items by project area or street section, state current status plainly, and give a target completion date for each open item. Confident and factual — acknowledge open items directly rather than minimizing them."*

**Data points → narrative analysis:**
> *"I have these project cost, schedule variance, and resident contact figures from the past three fiscal years. Write a coherent three-paragraph operational narrative suitable for an annual department report — covering trend, interpretation, and implications for next year's planning assumptions."*

**Regulatory provisions → resident-facing summary:**
> *"Convert this regulatory code extract into a clear, readable summary for residents with no government experience. Plain language, no jargon, highlight the three requirements most likely to affect a first-time permit applicant."*

**Meeting notes → project brief:**
> *"Convert these interagency meeting notes into a formal project brief for the department team. Sections: Project and Partner Context, Objectives, Program Requirements, Timeline and Milestones, Budget Parameters, Deliverables and Reporting. Note explicitly anything the partner agency stated as a hard requirement versus a preference."*

## 7. Multi-File Grounding — Referencing Up to 20 Sources in a Single Draft

One of the most powerful and least-understood capabilities of Copilot in Word is the ability to reference multiple source files simultaneously — up to 20 items — in a single draft prompt. This multi-file grounding is what separates Copilot from a general-purpose writing assistant and makes it genuinely useful for complex City documents.

:::{figure} ../images/ch06-multi-file-grounding.png
:label: fig-ch06-multifile
:alt: Hub-and-spoke diagram with a central Copilot Word document in the middle, surrounded by six source file icons connected by arrows — each source labeled with a City of Bowie document type: Field Assessment Notes (SharePoint), Regulatory Guidance Package (OneDrive), Prior After-Action Report (SharePoint), Budget Forecast (OneDrive), Intergovernmental Agreement (SharePoint), Project Kickoff Meeting Notes (OneDrive) — all flowing into the central document with a slash-reference symbol on each arrow, professional City of Bowie brand infographic style
:width: 80%

Up to 20 source files referenced in a single Copilot prompt — field assessment notes, regulatory guidance, prior after-action reports, budget forecasts, intergovernmental agreements, kickoff meeting notes — all grounding the draft in actual documents rather than general knowledge. This is the capability that makes operations manuals, grant applications, and multi-source project briefs genuinely efficient to produce.
:::

### Why Multi-File Grounding Matters

Consider what a complete project operations manual actually requires:

- The field assessment notes and photo log from the site walkthrough
- The regulatory requirements and permit conditions
- The intergovernmental agreement and any addenda
- The prior project's after-action report
- The budget authorization and funding source requirements
- The contractor's approved traffic control and staging plan
- The resident communication plan and notification schedule

Previously, a project manager would open each of these documents, read them, take notes, and then write the manual from those notes and their memory of what each document contained. The process was accurate — experienced City professionals are extremely good at this — but it was slow, and the quality depended heavily on one person's ability to hold multiple complex documents in mind simultaneously, usually while also managing active constituent inquiries and department meetings.

With multi-file grounding, you reference all relevant documents in your prompt. Copilot synthesizes across them in real time, producing a draft that is grounded in all of the source material simultaneously.

### The Workflow in Practice

**Setup:** Ensure all relevant documents are saved in SharePoint or OneDrive. Create a project folder in SharePoint for every significant engagement — this discipline of organizing project documents in SharePoint pays dividends not just for Copilot but for team collaboration, for the handoff between project phases, and for whoever inherits this project when staff transitions occur.

**Prompting:** When you are ready to draft the operations manual, invoke Copilot and use `/` to reference each source:

> *"Write a comprehensive project operations manual for the Kenilworth Avenue Corridor Rehabilitation, where the City of Bowie Department of Public Works is the lead. Use the following sources: / [field assessment notes], / [permit conditions and regulatory requirements], / [intergovernmental agreement with Prince George's County], / [prior project after-action report], / [budget authorization], / [contractor traffic control plan]. Structure the manual with: Project Overview and Key Dates, Team and Escalation Contacts, Site Access and Staging Assignments, Construction Phasing by Week, Traffic Management and Detour Procedures, Materials and Quality Standards, Resident Communication Schedule, Safety Protocols and Incident Reporting, Inspection and Acceptance Procedures, Project Closeout Requirements. Operational, directive register — written to be executed by the City project manager and contractor supervisors across all project phases."*

Copilot produces a draft that draws from all six referenced documents. The site access section will reflect the actual permit conditions. The traffic management section will reflect the actual approved contractor plan. The phasing schedule will reflect the actual intergovernmental agreement milestones. The risk notes will reflect what actually went wrong on a prior similar project.

**Your verification work:** Compare each section against its source document. Verify every date, location, and condition against the actual permit. Confirm every milestone against the actual intergovernmental agreement. Check that the phasing schedule is internally consistent with the traffic management restrictions — a common and expensive class of error that a document can carry all the way to construction. The multi-file grounding reduces the likelihood of Copilot inventing details, because it is working from your actual documents rather than its general training. But it does not eliminate the need for verification — it makes verification faster and more targeted, because you know exactly which source document to check for each section.

::::{admonition} ⚠️ Multi-File Prompting — What Can Go Wrong
:class: warning

Even with multi-file grounding, there are failure modes to watch for:

**Conflicting information across documents:** If the intergovernmental agreement shows a different construction completion date than the permit conditions, Copilot may pick one without flagging the discrepancy. Your job is to notice and resolve conflicts in the source material — and that particular conflict is exactly the kind that becomes a significant cost and schedule problem.

**Selective extraction:** Copilot extracts what it judges to be relevant to your prompt. If you ask for a construction sequence and there is a critical environmental protection requirement buried in an appendix, Copilot may not surface it if it does not seem sequencing-relevant. Read the source documents; do not rely solely on what Copilot extracted.

**Outdated source documents:** Referencing last year's permit conditions saved in SharePoint will produce a manual based on last year's requirements. Regulatory conditions and program requirements change. Maintain disciplined version control in your SharePoint project folders, and date-stamp reference documents in the file name.

**Silent gaps:** If a source document simply does not address something you asked about, Copilot may fill the gap with plausible general knowledge rather than telling you the gap exists. Add this to your prompts: *"If the source documents do not contain the information needed for a section, write [SOURCE GAP] in that section rather than generating content."*

## 8. When the Document Is Bigger Than One Session — Copilot Cowork

Everything so far in this chapter happens inside Word, in a session where you are present, steering each prompt. That is the right model for most document work. But there is a category of City of Bowie document work where it is not the right model — and that is where **Copilot Cowork** comes in.

Cowork became generally available worldwide on **June 16, 2026**, after being the fastest-growing feature in the history of Microsoft's Frontier early-access program. It is not a bigger version of Copilot in Word. It is a different way of working.

### The Distinction That Matters

| | **Copilot in Word** | **Copilot Cowork** |
|---|---|---|

That last row is not a footnote for City employees. It is the entire point. A new ADA accessibility section needs to be added to every constituent service guide. And there are fifteen active service guides across seven departments, each with its own SharePoint program folder, each with its own program-specific content that must not be touched.

> **Inputs:** The approved constituent service guide template in / [City SharePoint — Communications Templates], the new fee schedule effective [date], the process update memo dated [date], the ADA accessibility section copy block, and the existing service guide for each program in the department program folders under / [City Departments SharePoint].

## 9. The Verification Discipline — Every Paragraph Gets Human Eyes

We have covered a set of distinct capabilities. Now we come to the principle that makes all of them safe to use — and one that Microsoft has built directly into the product's design philosophy.

:label: fig-ch06-verification
:alt: Professional process flow diagram showing a Copilot draft document entering a verification checkpoint gate in the center — the gate is represented as a City employee reviewing the document with a checklist — followed by two possible outcomes on the right: a green 'Ready to Send' path and a red 'Revise' loop back to the document — the checklist inside the gate shows five items: Facts verified against source, Numbers independently confirmed, Interpretations reviewed, Regulatory and program language validated, Document read end-to-end by the professional — City of Bowie brand professional infographic

The verification discipline is the professional standard that makes AI-assisted work trustworthy. Microsoft explicitly calls for it in their own documentation. Every Copilot draft at the City of Bowie gets human eyes before it reaches a department director, a resident, or a partner agency — not as bureaucracy, but as professional responsibility.

### Microsoft's Own Words

It is worth pausing on something important: Microsoft itself has built the verification requirement into the product's design documentation. Their guidance on Copilot in Word states explicitly: *"Remember that Copilot generates a draft. You'll need to verify and modify details to make sure it's accurate and fits your tone and style."*

This is not a legal disclaimer buried in fine print. It is the core message of how Microsoft explains the product to users. The AI generates the draft. The human verifies and owns the final document. This is the intended workflow — not a workaround for AI limitations, but the correct division of labor between human expertise and AI assistance.

At the City of Bowie, the stakes make this more than a best practice. A wrong document does not get quietly corrected in a follow-up email. It gets executed. By contractors. By staff. By residents acting on information they trusted their City to provide correctly.

### Why Verification Is Non-Negotiable

**Fabrication is real.** Copilot can generate plausible-sounding fee amounts, regulatory deadlines, permit conditions, or program eligibility rules that are simply inaccurate. This is more likely when prompts are vague, when source documents are complex, or when Copilot is working from general knowledge rather than grounded source material. A constituent service guide with a wrong fee amount or a wrong application deadline is not a productivity win. It is hundreds of residents acting on incorrect information, and a Constituent Services team spending the next week correcting the record.

**Multi-file grounding reduces fabrication but does not eliminate it.** When you reference documents with `/`, Copilot works from real source material. This significantly improves accuracy for factual details. But Copilot can still misread a table, attach a figure to the wrong program, or mischaracterize a regulatory provision. Verification remains essential regardless of how well-grounded the prompt was.

**Nuance is yours to supply.** Copilot cannot know the history between the City and a specific partner agency. It cannot know that a particular intersection has an unresolved safety concern the department has been tracking. It cannot know that a specific funding source has an interpretation of an allowable cost that differs from the general program guidance, or that a particular community has a specific sensitivity about a project communication. Professional judgment supplies the context that makes documents reliable — not just grammatically correct.

**The sender is responsible.** Documents carry the implicit or explicit endorsement of the professional who sends them. The department manager who approves a service guide owns that document — whether Copilot wrote the first draft or not. The project manager who distributes an operations manual is accountable for every instruction in it. AI assists the process. It does not transfer the responsibility.

### The Five-Point Verification Checklist

:::{figure} ../images/ch06-verification-checklist.png
:label: fig-ch06-checklist
:alt: Clean desk-reference checklist card in City of Bowie brand colors — five items arranged vertically with checkbox icons: (1) Every fact verified against the source document, (2) Every number independently confirmed against the source, (3) Every interpretation reviewed for accuracy in this specific context, (4) Regulatory, program, and compliance language checked against current source, (5) Document read start to finish as if personally authored — footer text reads 'Every Copilot document. Every time.' — formatted like a printable laminated card, professional typography
:width: 80%
:align: center

Five items. Every document. Every time. Post this at your desk, save it to your OneDrive, or keep it in your project binder. The professional who applies this consistently is not slower than one who skips it — they are protected, and so are the residents who depend on what they produce.
:::

Apply this checklist to every Copilot-drafted document before it leaves your hands:

**1. Every fact verified against the source.** Specific facts — dates, addresses, fees, deadlines, contact names, program names, eligibility criteria — have been checked against the actual source document. No fact is assumed correct because it appeared in the Copilot draft.

**2. Every number independently confirmed.** Fees, weights, dimensions, deadlines, budget figures, and schedule dates have been verified against the actual source. Numbers are the most likely element to be misread or miscalculated in a Copilot draft. In City government they are also the most consequential for residents and contractors. Verify them every time.

**3. Every interpretation reviewed.** Where Copilot has added analytical language — "the project timeline is achievable given the available contractor capacity," "this fee structure aligns with comparable County programs" — you have confirmed that the interpretation is accurate for this specific project and program, not just generically reasonable.

**4. Regulatory, program, and compliance language validated.** Where the document references regulatory requirements, program eligibility rules, permit conditions, intergovernmental obligations, or compliance deadlines, a qualified professional has confirmed the reference is accurate and current. This is the highest-risk category in City documents, and it is the category where a generally-plausible answer is most dangerous, because it reads exactly like a correct one.

**5. The document is yours.** Before sending, publishing, or filing: read the document from beginning to end as if you wrote every word. If something does not sound right, does not read right, or would not survive a question from a resident at a community meeting — revise it. The document leaves your desk under your professional reputation. It should represent your judgment, not just Copilot's first attempt.

### Building the Habit

The verification discipline is a habit, and like all habits, it must be built deliberately before it becomes automatic.

For your first 30 days of using Copilot in Word, commit to applying the five-point checklist explicitly — checking each item consciously, not just scanning through the document once. This will feel slower than you expect at first. That is normal. You are building the mental model for what kinds of outputs Copilot produces reliably and where your verification attention needs to concentrate.

After 30 days of consistent verification, you will have developed professional intuition for Copilot's patterns: document structure is almost always right; prose quality is generally high; tone calibrations are usually close; specific numbers, dates, regulatory requirements, and program eligibility conditions require careful independent confirmation every single time.

That intuition is what makes you an effective Copilot user. Not blind trust, and not blanket skepticism — calibrated professional judgment. The same judgment you already apply to a project schedule, a budget estimate, or a grant application that someone else prepared.

### The Monday Morning Protocol

**Before you start:** Make sure the relevant source documents are saved to SharePoint or OneDrive. If you are working from a project folder, confirm it is organized and current. Documents that live only on your local drive — or on a thumb drive in a project binder — cannot be referenced by Copilot.

**Choosing the right capability:**

- Starting from nothing with only a set of facts in your head → **Start a Draft**, with a rich five-element prompt
- Starting from field assessment notes, regulatory packages, prior project documents already in SharePoint → **Base on Existing File**, referencing up to 20 items with `/`
- Your document is underway and needs a new section or an expanded section → **Add to Existing Document**
- You have a paragraph that isn't working → **Rewrite** the selected text with a follow-up prompt
- The job spans many files, many programs, or many departments — and you would rather review finished work than steer every step → **Cowork**, with an outcome-shaped assignment

**The refinement loop:** After Copilot produces its first output, use follow-up prompts rather than editing manually where possible. *"Make the recommendation section more direct"* or *"The project phasing section is too long — condense to a table plus two paragraphs"* or *"Adjust the tone to be warmer — this is going to residents who have been waiting a long time for this project"* — each of these gives Copilot a chance to improve the draft before you invest editing time.

**The verification gate:** Before any document moves to its next stage — department director review, resident distribution, contractor briefing, partner agency submission, or the project file — apply the five-point checklist. Every time.

**The time math:** A project operations manual that previously required a full day now requires 90 minutes to two hours: ten minutes of prompt preparation, two minutes of generation, and the balance in verification, professional enrichment, and refinement. The time is not eliminated — it is reallocated from scaffolding to judgment. That reallocation is the real productivity gain, and across a City serving 70,000+ residents with finite staff capacity, it compounds into something significant for the people we serve.

## Chapter Summary

The blank page is no longer the enemy. Copilot in Word provides three verified, production-ready capabilities: **Start a Draft** when you are beginning from nothing; **Base on Existing File** when you have source material already in SharePoint or OneDrive, referenceable with `/` for up to 20 items; and **Add to Existing Document** when your draft is underway and needs expansion. The Rewrite capability handles the revision side — transforming selected passages, adjusting tone, and tightening prose through a simple refinement prompt.

Multi-file grounding — referencing multiple documents simultaneously in a single prompt — is the capability that makes complex City documents efficient to produce. A project operations manual that previously required reading seven source documents independently and synthesizing from notes can now be drafted from those seven documents referenced directly, with Copilot synthesizing across all of them in real time.

Summarization at scale gives City staff a 90-minute task back: an 80-page regulatory package condensed to a structured one-page compliance summary, with every material requirement extracted and organized for review. The professional's work shifts from reading to verification — which is a better use of trained expertise.

Tone adjustment enables the same facts to reach City leadership, a grant funder or partner agency, and a resident in the language each audience needs. The professional's judgment ensures that the register is not just structurally correct but contextually accurate — that the leadership version reflects what *this* City Council cares about, not what councils generically care about.

Consistency across the City is the capability that is uniquely valuable at the City of Bowie's shape and scope. With seven departments, hundreds of active programs and projects, and 70,000+ residents to serve, the same document types are produced continuously by different staff in different departments. Copilot, pointed at a well-governed set of approved reference documents, standardizes structure and tone while explicitly preserving what is legitimately specific — program eligibility rules, fee schedules, regulatory conditions, and department-specific operating practices.

Copilot Cowork extends all of it beyond a single Word session. Cowork creates documents from scratch, edits existing files, browses OneDrive and SharePoint on its own, and returns finished artifacts saved where you asked — while your laptop is closed and you are in the field. The skill shifts from describing a task to describing an outcome and then reviewing like a manager.

And underlying all of it: the verification discipline. Microsoft itself states in their documentation: *"You'll need to verify and modify details to make sure it's accurate and fits your tone and style."* At the City of Bowie, the stakes make this more than a guideline. The five-point checklist — facts, numbers, interpretations, regulatory and program language, and end-to-end ownership — is the professional standard that makes AI-assisted work trustworthy, defensible, and worthy of the residents who depend on it.

The AI generates the draft. The professional generates the judgment. That division of labor is the design. And it is the right one.

```{glossary}
Start a Draft (Copilot in Word)
  The Copilot in Word capability that generates a full structured document draft from a user prompt in a new or empty document — addressing the blank-page starting challenge for professional City documents.

Base on Existing File (Copilot in Word)
  The Copilot in Word capability that grounds a new document draft in specific source files — Word documents, emails, meeting transcripts, and other content saved in SharePoint or OneDrive — referenced using the `/` slash command.

Add to Existing Document (Copilot in Word)
  The Copilot in Word capability that generates new content within an already-started document — adding sections, expanding existing sections, or producing content that matches the register and structure of what is already written.

Rewrite (Copilot in Word)
  The Copilot in Word capability that generates alternative versions of selected text — enabling tone adjustment, register improvement, and prose refinement through iterative follow-up prompts.

Slash Reference
  The `/` mechanism in Copilot prompts that opens a file picker, allowing users to reference up to 20 SharePoint or OneDrive files as grounding sources for a Copilot-generated draft.

Multi-File Grounding
  The practice of referencing multiple source documents in a single Copilot prompt using `/` — producing a draft that synthesizes across all referenced sources simultaneously rather than relying on general AI knowledge.

Copilot Cowork
  The Microsoft 365 Copilot capability, generally available June 16, 2026, that executes long-running, multi-step work across apps in a secure cloud environment and returns finished artifacts — including Word documents created from scratch, edits to existing files, and reorganized SharePoint and OneDrive folders — with tasks continuing to run while the user's device is off.

Verification Discipline
  The professional practice of applying a five-point checklist — facts, numbers, interpretations, regulatory and program language, and end-to-end ownership — to every AI-generated document before it leaves the professional's hands, as explicitly recommended by Microsoft's own product documentation.

Hallucination
  The AI phenomenon in which a model generates factually incorrect information with apparent confidence — including inaccurate fees, mischaracterized regulatory requirements, or fabricated deadlines — requiring human verification of all factual outputs regardless of how grounded the prompt was.

Refinement Loop
  The iterative process of improving a Copilot draft through follow-up prompts (such as "Make it more concise" or "Adjust the tone for a first-time permit applicant") before committing to manual editing — a more efficient path to a high-quality draft than editing the first output directly.

Register
  The level and style of language appropriate to a specific professional context or audience — from formal regulatory language in a compliance report, to operational and directive language in a project operations manual, to warm plain-spoken language in a resident service guide.

Constituent Service Guide
  The document the City of Bowie provides to residents and businesses describing a City service — containing process steps, fees, deadlines, contact information, eligibility requirements, and relevant regulatory references. One of the highest-volume and most consistency-sensitive document types the City produces.

Department SOP (Standard Operating Procedure)
  The internal operational document governing how a City department performs a specific function — covering step-by-step procedures, responsible roles, compliance requirements, and escalation protocols — written to be executed by department staff consistently across all service interactions.

After-Action Report
  The document produced after a project, event, or incident is complete that assesses what was planned versus what actually occurred, documents lessons learned, and captures recommendations for future similar projects or programs.

Scope of Work (SOW)
  The contractual document defining precisely which services a vendor or contractor provides for a City engagement, which are excluded, and the assumptions and dependencies on which the plan rests.

City of Bowie Official Voice
  The City of Bowie's document communication standard: professional, clear, and accessible — reflecting the City's commitment to Accountability, Responsiveness, Stewardship, and Pride in all written communications to residents, partner agencies, and the public.
```
